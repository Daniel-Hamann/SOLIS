Chapter 07

Education and Knowledge

Education as a Right and a Foundation

In SOLIS, education is a fundamental human right, as indispensable as water, food, and security — not a luxury, not a business, and not an instrument of power. This commitment follows from the insight that education is far more than passing on knowledge. It means access to information, the capacity for reflection, the chance to develop oneself, and the key to self-determination, participation, and dignity. Without it, people remain dependent and vulnerable, poverty reproduces itself from generation to generation, and inequality is handed down. Whoever secures education secures all other rights along with it; whoever denies it takes away the foundation of people’s freedom.

Access is free and barrier-free. Every person has a right to education from birth, because education does not begin at school but in the family and the community, and it does not end with a diploma but continues throughout life. An expectant mother has access to counselling and learning materials; a young person with a disability is given learning systems that build on their strengths and compensate for their obstacles; an older person can keep learning new languages, techniques, and subjects free of charge.

Because not everyone learns in the same way, the education system adapts. It takes different learning styles into account, respects cultural diversity, and sees disabilities not as deficits but as variations of human experience; digital platforms, shared learning spaces, and professional support ensure that content is prepared flexibly and tailored to particular needs. Artificial obstacles disappear. There are no fees that reserve education for the wealthy, no admission barriers that make opportunities depend on background and connections, and no discrimination that systematically disadvantages minorities. A highly gifted young woman from a poor household does not have to go into debt to study, and a worker who wants to change direction at fifty is not blocked by bureaucracy.

All this is carried by a publicly funded educational infrastructure of schools, libraries, learning centres, and digital platforms, because education counts as a collective gain and not as a commodity. Its content is scientifically grounded, ethically reflected, and presented pluralistically. This sets a limit from the outset, to which the following sections return: no indoctrination, no propaganda, and no dictate of “correct” opinions.

Lifelong Learning

In most of today’s societies, learning is organized as a closed phase of life: first school, then training, then working life — and after that, a person counts as finished. In a rapidly changing world, this idea is disastrous, because standing still means falling behind, and yesterday’s knowledge is not enough to solve tomorrow’s problems. SOLIS starts from the assumption that learning never stops — not because people should optimize themselves without pause, but because learning is part of being human, an expression of curiosity, growth, and connection.

Everyone therefore has lifelong access to a wide range of learning opportunities — formal institutions as well as informal formats and self-directed paths. A tradesperson who wants to train in sustainable architecture at forty finds open courses and mentors; someone returning after years away is offered programmes that bring them up to date. Learning here is neither a stigma nor a status symbol, but a natural part of life, and no one is smiled at for still learning in old age, or excluded because their learning happens informally.

For this to be possible, the world of work has to encourage learning rather than penalize it. In many of today’s jobs, further training is a private burden, with no time and no money for it; in SOLIS, employers are obliged to enable continuous learning, working hours are arranged so that there is room for phases of learning, and new roles and careers arise from lifelong development rather than from a one-off diploma. An experienced carer who wants to move into robotics is supported, not held back. Phases of learning are financially secured, so that no one has to go into debt for their education, and informal learning — projects, experience, contributions to the community — is recognized rather than measured against formal diplomas alone. The result is a society that stays curious at every age and grows wiser and more adaptable together.

Access to Knowledge and Transparency

In the information age, knowledge is more than power; it is the foundation of survival, innovation, and freedom. And yet in most of today’s systems it is not free, but hidden behind paywalls, locked up in patents, hoarded in data monopolies, and exploited by governments and corporations alike. SOLIS treats knowledge as a common good — not out of romanticism but out of necessity, because a society that shares knowledge is more resilient, more innovative, and more just.

To this end, SOLIS maintains open, multilingual, and barrier-free knowledge systems that make scientific findings, technological developments, historical sources, and cultural works accessible, complemented by local knowledge centres, so that people without digital infrastructure also have access. A village community can thus draw on the same medical knowledge as a research laboratory, and a young person can use the teaching materials of the best universities. What was paid for by the public belongs to the public: the studies, data, and methods of publicly funded research are open to all, which multiplies innovation, avoids needless duplication, and builds trust.

Private innovation remains protected, but not at the expense of the public. SOLIS recognizes that innovations from the private sector are valuable and grants them protection; yet patents and licences may never be designed in a way that endangers the common good, and in areas such as medicine, environmental technology, and basic provision, the public interest comes first. A company may patent an active ingredient, but in a global health crisis it must offer fair licensing models. Knowledge thus does not become a commodity but remains a vital resource open to all — an application of the same transparency that SOLIS demands of its own institutions.

Creativity and Critical Thinking

In many of today’s education systems, the hidden message is that you should absorb what you are given and reproduce it correctly; what is rewarded is conformity, not deviation. In an increasingly complex world, that is not enough. A society needs people who see new paths, question old patterns, and look at problems from unfamiliar angles. SOLIS therefore understands education not as a warehouse of knowledge, but as a space for thinking and exploring, in which what matters is not only the what, but equally the how and the why.

From an early age, children therefore learn not only facts but methods: how to analyse a situation, recognize patterns and contradictions, ask precise questions, and distinguish superficial causes from deeper ones. A historical event is not simply tested but worked through — why it happened, which perspectives existed, what might have gone differently, and what parallels it has with the present. Creativity counts here not as decoration but as a core skill, in art and science as much as in the economy and everyday life: the ability to connect the unexpected, to tolerate uncertainty, and to try something new without fear of failure. An education system that works this way does not fill vessels but lights a fire; it allows students to challenge their teachers, lets projects fail and still be valuable, and measures not how much knowledge has been ticked off but whether students find their own solutions.

The Maturity to Question Even SOLIS. Here lies a commitment that is decisive for the whole work. Critical thinking that stops short of the existing order is not critical thinking, but its opposite. Education in SOLIS therefore explicitly teaches people to question every authority — including SOLIS itself, its institutions, and its officeholders. It gives people not only the values of the order, but also the tools to judge whether the order lives up to those values, and to change or abolish it if it does not. This is precisely the strongest protection against any order hardening into dogma, and against the manipulation of the public sphere discussed in the section on the media: a population that has learned to check sources, compare perspectives, and question power cannot be dominated. SOLIS asks of its citizens not obedience but judgment — and in doing so accepts that it will be measured by that judgment and, if necessary, rejected.

Ethical Education

In an age in which technology, power, and knowledge create ever greater levers, it is not enough to treat ethics as a minor subject. Today, a single person can paralyse systems, alter genetic code, or influence the lives of millions through supply chains and data flows. If these levers are operated by people who think only of themselves, the result is injustice and destruction. SOLIS therefore makes ethical education not a special programme, but a foundation that runs through every field.

Ethical reflection is built into every discipline. In the natural sciences it concerns responsibility for the environment and for consequences; in economics, fairness and sustainability; in medicine, patients’ rights and fair access; in technology, the question of misuse. Every subject includes reflection on responsibility, and every project thinks through its ethical consequences, not only its benefits. This education starts early: children learn to consider the consequences of their actions, to put themselves in others’ shoes, and to take responsibility — by making rules together, resolving conflicts, and talking about real dilemmas. And it makes ethical discussion the normal case — in schools, the media, and neighbourhoods — because a healthy society does not shy away from questions such as what a good life is, what we owe future generations, and where freedom ends; it needs them. Education, Not Conditioning. This raises an objection that SOLIS takes seriously and does not gloss over. A society that raises its young in the values it lives by is always in danger of letting education slide into conditioning — of turning the path to maturity into drilling for compliance. SOLIS refuses to take that step, because it would be exactly the authoritarian move the whole order is fighting against. The line runs at a single point: a society may educate its children in the values that sustain it, but only in a way that preserves their ability to examine and reject those values. Ethical education in SOLIS therefore does not implant fixed “correct” convictions, but develops independent moral judgment — the ability to measure even SOLIS against its own standards. Formation that leaves a person free is education; formation that binds them is indoctrination. Only the first is compatible with the foundations of this order.

Global Knowledge Networks

The great questions of this century — climate, energy, health, peace, technology — know no borders and demand global answers. Such answers emerge only when knowledge flows freely across countries, cultures, and disciplines. In SOLIS, schools, universities, and research centres are therefore not isolated islands but part of a global network that shares data and methods, runs joint projects, and forms communities of learners and teachers across borders. A school on one continent works with a school on another on a reforestation project, combining local knowledge of plants, climate data, and technology into a model that works in both regions.

In SOLIS, “global” does not mean “Western,” but diverse and mutually enriching. The knowledge that counts is not only that of the great universities, but also that of Indigenous communities about ecosystems, of craft traditions about sustainable production, and of artistic movements about expression and change. A clean drinking-water project can thus combine high technology, traditional methods, and clever design into a solution that is adapted to local conditions, applicable worldwide, and culturally respectful. In this way, people learn not only about other cultures but with them and from them, and the intercultural judgment grows that builds trust where others put up walls. Knowledge becomes a global web of lights that illuminate one another.
