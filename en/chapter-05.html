Chapter 05

Economy and Resources

Property and Common Goods

Property is one of the most powerful and at the same time most contested ideas in human history. It has built societies and destroyed them, spurred innovation and fuelled wars, made freedom possible and legitimized oppression. Capitalism often treats property as freedom itself: whoever owns has power, security, and the ability to shape things. Socialism has often collectivized property, on the assumption that private ownership breeds inequality and destroys community — and has frequently ended in bureaucratic rigidity, a lack of incentives, and corruption in the administration. SOLIS does not swap one for the other, but seeks a new balance that uses the strengths of both principles and avoids their weaknesses: personal possessions remain inviolable, while the natural foundations of life are treated as common goods. In SOLIS, personal possessions are protected because they are part of self-determination. Your own house, your clothes, your tools, your savings, your books, your keepsakes, and above all everything you have made yourself belong to you; you may keep them, shape them, pass them on, and bequeath them. Possessions in this sense foster creativity, identity, and security: an artist keeps full rights to their works, just as a family keeps the right to live in the house it has built and to pass it on to its children.

The great foundations of life are a different matter: water, soil, air, energy sources, and raw materials. They cannot belong to anyone privately — because no one created them, because they are the condition for everyone’s survival, and because their misuse harms not only individuals but entire societies, ecosystems, and generations. In SOLIS, therefore, no company can acquire a water source and deny people access to it, no investor can buy up land and leave it idle for speculation while people cannot find housing, and no corporation can treat the air as if it were a commodity.

These common goods are managed collectively — and, in keeping with the order of power, not as state property, but through a combination of citizen participation, scientific expertise, and ethical review. Their use is regulated so that no one is starved out or exploited and future generations keep their access too. The tools for this are open platforms that show how much water, energy, or soil is being used where; sustainability standards that set how much may be extracted without harming the system; sanctions against the build-up of monopolies and shadow markets; and innovation programmes that promote sustainable use. SOLIS thus resolves the false choice between boundless private power that destroys the collective and total collective control that suffocates the individual. It grants freedom where freedom fosters creativity, innovation, and identity, and collective management where survival and sustainability are at stake. What grows from this is not only balance but trust: individuals know that they have their own space and security, and at the same time that the foundations of life will remain open to their children as well.

Money, Value, and Cycles

For centuries, money has been one of humanity’s central tools. It has made trade possible and triggered crises, fuelled innovation and plunged societies into poverty, promised freedom and created dependency. Under capitalism, money often becomes the measure of all things: whoever has it can buy influence, secure their safety, and help make the rules. Socialism has often suppressed or tightly controlled money, and in doing so frequently produced bureaucratic rigidity, scarcity, and black markets. SOLIS does not simply replace money with a new means of exchange. It creates a two-part order with a clear separation between basic provision and discretionary goods.

Basic Provision Is an Entitlement, Not a Payment

In SOLIS, every person has a guaranteed entitlement to the foundations of life: housing, energy, food, health care, education, and basic mobility. These are not market goods; they are provided through planning, capacity, and shared contributions, and no one ever has to pay for them. A young adult is thus entitled to a basic home without having to give anything in return, and a family receives staple foods as an entitlement, not in exchange for payment. This secures a floor below which no one can fall, regardless of their employment history.

Value Points for Discretionary Goods

Everything beyond basic provision is obtained with value points. They are tied to the person and bear no interest; they arise solely from real contribution — from work, care, raising children, innovation, and voluntary service; they cannot be hoarded or traded speculatively; and their relative worth reflects social benefit, sustainability, and scarcity. An engineer receives value points for working on an energy plant, a carer for looking after people day by day, an artist for their work. What matters is what sets such a medium apart from money: it cannot turn itself into capital that multiplies and translates into power.

How Prices Arise without a Central Plan

This is the hardest question for any economic order that pushes back the market, and SOLIS does not dodge it. A price is information. In a market it arises from countless decentralized decisions and signals what is scarce and what is needed. An order that abolishes the market must say how, without this signal, it intends to determine what things are worth — and history has shown that no central authority can calculate what a market computes. SOLIS does not claim this omniscience. It does not replace the market with a central plan; it sets boundaries for it.

For discretionary goods, relative prices in value points are not decreed from above but emerge from decentralized signals, in three layers. The first layer is the signals themselves: providers and consumers register supply and demand, and relative prices move with actual scarcity and actual demand. This preserves the information function of prices, which a pure plan loses. The second layer is fixed limits: ecological costs are built into prices by rule, so that a flight across continents costs more points than a train journey, and an environmentally harmful product more than a sustainable alternative; at the same time, no one can corner a market or build a monopoly, and work in areas of shortage earns more points per hour. The third layer is feedback: the entire system is transparent and open to review, and where the signals lead to unjust or unsustainable results, a correction loop kicks in that combines data and deliberation — continuous monitoring of scarcity and ecological strain on one side, ethical review on the other. What gets corrected are the limits, not individual prices by decree. This solution is an approximation, not a perfect calculation, and it presents itself as such. It accepts a degree of imprecision in allocation, because that is the price of avoiding both dead ends: the omniscient plan, which is impossible, and the unfettered market, which becomes unjust. The nature of the value point ensures that even where a price remains imprecise, no lever for accumulation and power can arise from it, because it cannot be hoarded, cannot be speculated with, and is tied to real contribution.

Innovation without the Barrier of Capital

Research and development must not founder for lack of capital. Central funds provide infrastructure, materials, and teams; personal effort is rewarded with value points; and ideas and inventions remain freely accessible, while the inventor receives recognition and a return in value points. A scientist who develops a new recycling process draws resources from the fund, their team is supported, and they are honoured for their contribution — without the invention being locked away behind a patent that denies the public access.

The Interface with the Outside World

SOLIS does not exist in isolation and therefore needs regulated crossings to the world of conventional currencies. A border exchange allows value points to be exchanged for foreign currencies. Yet so that the core property of the value point — no accumulation, no speculation — is not undermined from outside, this border is a controlled membrane, not an open gate for arbitrage. Exchange by individuals is limited: it covers legitimate external needs such as travel or imports, but does not allow points to be siphoned off into wealth abroad. Large-scale outflows that would turn value points into foreign capital and re-import power through it are visible and capped. A buffer fund keeps the exchange rate within a stable band and protects against external shocks and speculation. A company that exports technology receives value points that can be exchanged at the border within these rules. The whole purpose of the border is to protect the internal order against the detour through foreign currency: the membrane lets value pass for real needs and seals the leak through which someone could escape the rule against accumulation.

Transparency and Security

All transfers run through community banks, which serve not profit but secure settlement, transparency, and protection against misuse. The data sit on a tamper-proof infrastructure, personal privacy remains protected, and society sees only aggregate figures. In this way, the economic order ends dependence on money while keeping the benefits of regulated markets: no one need fear being unable to meet their basic needs; no one can establish a monopoly over air, water, or food; and no innovation fails any longer for lack of capital. Entitlements for survival and value points for shaping one’s life — that is where the balance, the trust, and the stability of this order lie.

Old-Age Security

How a society treats old age shows whether it values its people or abandons them. In many existing systems, provision for old age is shaped by fear — the fear of not having enough in old age, of ending up poor after a lifetime of work, or of becoming a burden on the community. Traditional pension systems are under pressure from demographic change, uncertain funding, and the failure to recognize a lifetime’s contribution fairly. SOLIS, by contrast, guarantees dignity in old age not as charity but as a right: no one falls below a defined level of dignity, those who have contributed receive demonstrably more, and the system stays stable even as demographics shift.

This security rests on three pillars. The first is a basic pension which, through the entitlements to housing, energy, health care, and food, secures a dignified life for everyone, regardless of their employment history. The second is earned points, which arise from work, care, raising children, and voluntary service. The third is a productive fund — a collective, broadly diversified fund invested in the real economy and infrastructure, whose returns flow to older people as value points. Because security never rests on a single source, it holds even when one source weakens. What matters is that a lifetime’s contribution counts in every form. Caring for relatives and raising children are fully credited, not as a gap in one’s CV but as a valuable contribution, so that a mother who spends years raising children builds up points just as someone in paid work does. A qualification-and-shortage factor gives extra weight to especially needed work, while a cap protects against self-exploitation. A sustainability factor gently evens out demographic swings: the basic pension is funded from the community budget, earned points run through an active pay-as-you-go mechanism, and the productive fund is broadly diversified and invested counter-cyclically.

The system is also flexible and humane. A gradual transition into retirement, say between the ages of 62 and 70, allows for individual paths with a fairness factor; those who want to work longer are rewarded for it, and those who have to stop earlier do not fall into the void. In the event of reduced earning capacity, notional credits provide security, and surviving dependants are protected by the transfer of fund shares. The transition from the existing system is designed fairly as well: acquired entitlements are converted, a long period with both systems in parallel ensures a gentle changeover, and older cohorts receive guaranteed minimums. SOLIS thus takes the fear out of old age — no poverty in old age after a lifetime of work, no penalty for caring and raising children, no collapse through demographic imbalance.

Security and Planetary Defence

Throughout history, armed power has always been double-edged: it has offered protection and brought destruction, secured stability and stirred up fear, defended peoples and unleashed wars. In traditional systems, the military often serves national interests — projecting power, securing resources, gaining geopolitical influence — and so leads to wars of aggression, arms races, and oppression at home. SOLIS sees defence not as an instrument of attack or expansion, but purely as a structure that protects people, infrastructure, and the planet.

The doctrine is strictly defensive. Defence protects the population from attack, secures critical infrastructure and data, and preserves ecosystems and resources; an attack on the power grid, for example, is repelled through cyber defence without SOLIS itself escalating militarily. The system is organized in tiers — cyber and information, air and space, land and sea defence, and civil protection — whose specialized forces act together in coordination. Civil protection is integrated with the military structures, so that dedicated units can respond flexibly to natural disasters, pandemics, technological accidents, and threats from space: after an earthquake they support evacuations, provide emergency power, and secure medical care; and an asteroid detected early is deflected while civil protection prepares evacuations in parallel.

Above all stand ethics and oversight. There are no offensive missions and no autonomous weapon systems without human control; an artificial intelligence may analyse threats but may not make a lethal decision without human authorization. The military leadership is always under civilian control and thus, like every form of power in SOLIS, under the oversight of the Ring and subject to the law. To prevent misuse, escalation follows a clear, public model that runs from the normal state through heightened vigilance and partial mobilization to full mobilization; each stage comes with clearly defined powers, and every escalation is followed by a duty to de-escalate. Internationally, SOLIS pursues a balance of caution through shared early-warning systems, arms control, and emergency protocols between states. In this way, it frees defence from the logic of attack and power projection: no arms race, no war of aggression, no deployment against its own population — only the protection of people and the planet.

Value Creation, Innovation, and Work

In most of today’s systems, work is closely tied to fear — the fear of not earning enough, of not being needed, of falling behind, or of becoming ill and burnt out and therefore worthless. We have built societies in which work is above all a means of survival, and the result is people who stay in jobs they do not care for, creativity that suffocates under daily drudgery, and innovations that lie fallow because they do not pay off in the short term. SOLIS understands work differently: not as a necessity for survival, but as an expression of creativity, contribution, and meaning.

Because the foundations of life are guaranteed in SOLIS, no one has to work in order to survive. This does not lead to no one working any more, but to the opposite: people get involved because they see meaning in it, want to contribute, and want to grow. A young mother teaches part-time because she enjoys passing on knowledge, not out of fear of otherwise losing her security; an older engineer works on a sustainable energy innovation because he wants to do something meaningful, not because he is worried about the rent.

Technology serves to relieve people, not to replace them. Routine tasks, as well as dangerous or physically heavy work, are automated, so that people can focus on what machines cannot do: creativity, empathy, strategic thinking, complex problem-solving, and social and emotional work. Innovation is fostered as a collective good; public funds and support systems, networks of mentors and experts, and accessible innovation spaces are open to everyone, not only to large corporations or privileged elites — so that, for instance, a schoolchild with an idea for plastic-free packaging can get into an innovation centre without depending on wealthy parents or investors.

Companies continue to exist, because they are important drivers of dynamism, efficiency, and specialization. Yet their success is no longer measured by profit alone, but equally by economic stability, social contribution, ecological sustainability, and ethical responsibility, and a public, transparent rating system makes these dimensions visible. A company that pays fair wages, uses environmentally sound materials, and strengthens local communities is therefore rated higher than one that only cuts costs. The aim is an economy that does not move numbers around but creates real value — for the people who work in it, for the communities it serves, for the nature it uses and protects, and for the future it helps to shape. The point is not to maximize growth for its own sake, but to let the right things grow: health, education, creativity, sustainability, and justice.

Distributive Justice

Almost every political debate talks about justice, and yet everyone means something different by it — some absolute equality, others absolute freedom, still others a fair balance. In SOLIS, distributive justice does not mean that everyone receives exactly the same. It means three things: that everyone has enough for a dignified, secure, and fulfilled life; that those who put in particular effort may enjoy more, as long as they do not live at others’ expense; and that extreme inequalities that split society are actively prevented. This path follows from a twofold insight: radical equality often stifles achievement and innovation; radical inequality poisons societies, destroys trust, and breeds violence. The foundation is that no one has to fear for their bare survival. Access to food, clean water, medical care, secure housing, education, and participation is guaranteed — not as charity but as a basic right — so that a single mother does not have to worry about whether she can feed and provide for her children, and an old person does not fall into nothingness. This is the condition for every further freedom, because someone struggling to survive cannot think freely, be creative, or develop.

Above this foundation, SOLIS recognizes that people differ in talent, drive, and ability, and those who contribute a great deal, take on responsibility, or drive innovation may also enjoy more. The only thing that matters is that this does not rest on exploitation: you may be wealthy because you have created something, not because you have oppressed or cheated others. What is rewarded, then, is the scientist with a medical breakthrough, the entrepreneur who creates fair jobs and sustainable products, and the artist who enriches the culture — but not the speculator who makes money from money without creating value, the corporation that offloads environmental damage onto the public, or the elite that defines itself solely through power and exclusivity.

So that the extremes do not arise in the first place, SOLIS builds in balancing mechanisms. Progressive taxation asks those who have more for a proportionally higher contribution — not as a punishment, but in recognition that wealth always also rests on the community: on its infrastructure, its education and health systems, and its stability. The money raised does not disappear into opaque coffers, but is invested visibly and verifiably in education, health, innovation, and community. Innovation bonuses give those who contribute ideas of long-term social benefit not only recognition but also the means to realize them. The order thus combines the security people need to live free of fear with the dynamism societies need to move forward. The point is not to demonize wealth, but to tie it to responsibility and to prevent extreme poverty, because poverty paralyses not only those affected but society as a whole. Where people experience the system as fair, trust grows — and trust is the glue without which the economy, politics, and community fall apart.

Sustainability and the Circular Economy

The conventional economy follows a destructive pattern: take, use, throw away. Raw materials are extracted, turned into products, consumed, and finally disposed of as waste. This linear system has left behind exhausted soils, polluted seas, toxic landfills, and destroyed ecosystems, and it has accelerated climate change. SOLIS breaks with this logic and builds an economy that does not merely destroy less, but actively regenerates and heals — cycles instead of lines, partnership instead of exploitation, long-term viability instead of short-term gain. In a circular economy, resources are not used up but kept in circulation. Materials are used for as long as possible, reused, and recycled to a high standard; by-products serve as inputs for other processes; waste counts not as rubbish but as a resource. A discarded device casing is not incinerated but taken apart, cleaned, and turned into new products, and industries are linked so that the residues of one become the raw materials of another. This goes beyond recycling; it is a system designed for circulation from the start.

Energy comes almost entirely from renewable sources — sun, wind, water, and geothermal heat — and SOLIS also invests specifically in advanced technologies such as fusion energy, highly efficient storage, and smart grids that balance fluctuations and optimize energy flows, so that fossil fuels are replaced completely. Products are durable, repairable, modular, and recyclable; planned obsolescence is abolished. A device has standardized modules, so that when the battery weakens, only the battery is replaced and not the whole device, and at the end of its life the individual parts return to the cycle. Agriculture, forestry, and fishing also work with nature rather than against it: soils are regenerated through crop rotation, building up humus, and agroforestry; forests are managed sustainably; and fish stocks are used only to the extent that they remain stable in the long term.

SOLIS thus goes beyond merely reducing harm and aims at active healing — at reforestation that restores entire ecosystems, at the renaturation of rivers, and at processes that not only avoid carbon emissions but remove carbon from the atmosphere and return it to the soil. In this way, the order breaks with the idea that the economy is a zero-sum game in which nature loses whatever the economy gains. In its place comes a system in which prosperity and the protection of nature go hand in hand, in which innovation builds up rather than destroys, and in which cities can, in the long run, produce more energy than they consume.

Technology and Automation

Since time immemorial, human beings have built tools to help them, from the hand axe to the quantum computer. Yet every tool raises a question: who is serving whom? Does technology free us or make us dependent, does it create prosperity or deepen inequality, does it make work easier or strip it of meaning? SOLIS faces these questions and uses technology deliberately as a tool for relief, improvement, and healing — not as an uncontrolled force that replaces, manipulates, or dominates people.

Automation is used where it relieves, not where it destroys. It reduces physical strain, takes over monotonous routine, and improves quality and safety, creating room for creativity, empathy, and innovation. In agriculture, automated systems support precision irrigation and soil analysis, while designing crop plans and caring for ecosystems remain in human hands; in care work, machines take over heavy lifting and monitoring, while attention, conversation, and warmth remain the preserve of human beings. So that automation does not cause mass unemployment, SOLIS funds further training, retraining, and the development of talent, and guides people specifically into fields where their abilities are needed.

Powerful emerging technologies — artificial intelligence, robotics, biotechnology — are ethically examined and actively steered. Every new technology is assessed not only for its economic benefit, but for its social, ecological, and psychological consequences; techniques such as gene editing, quantum computing, or neural interfaces are not left to market forces alone, but accompanied by ethical guidelines and public debate; and dedicated institutions — technology councils, ethics centres, and citizens’ forums — help decide what is developed, what is rolled out, and what must be stopped or changed. An artificial intelligence used in health care must therefore be not only reliable but also able to explain how it reaches its decisions; it remains understandable and is not a black box.

Above all stands a clear basic stance: technology should make people stronger, freer, and more creative, not weaker, more dependent, and more uniform. Human beings remain sovereign. Machines do not steer people; people steer machines. Algorithms do not write the laws; people write algorithms that respect the laws. And biotechnology does not determine the future of our species; human beings decide which interventions are ethically justifiable. What we decide in the coming decades will help determine whether humanity slides into a world of surveillance and dehumanization or rises into a world of possibility and new forms of meaning. SOLIS takes the helm: it uses technology without letting itself be used by it.

Global Economic Cooperation

We live in a world bound together by the economy; no country and no region stands alone any more. Raw materials, goods, data, and ideas flow across borders, and that can be a blessing or a curse. So far the curse has prevailed: free trade that maximizes profits but destroys local communities; global supply chains built on exploitation and environmental destruction; and geopolitical power games in which the economy is misused as a means of pressure. SOLIS also thinks about the economy globally — but not as plunder; rather as a cooperative network that benefits everyone and not only the strongest.

Trade relations rest on fairness, sustainability, and respect. Agreements are not power games between industrial giants, but arrangements based on clear ethical principles: fair pay, ecological responsibility, respect for cultural differences, and long-term partnership instead of short-term extraction. A corporation from a rich country may therefore not simply extract cheap raw materials from a poorer one without compensating local communities and meeting ecological standards; contracts must disclose how investment is made on the ground, which standards apply, and how both sides benefit.

Emerging and developing countries are strengthened rather than kept dependent. Instead of aid that entrenches dependency, there are transfers of technology and knowledge, fair financing for infrastructure that builds local resilience, and partnerships between equals in which local voices have a say. A country can thus develop its own sustainable energy systems with the support of global networks, but without binding contracts that plunge it into endless debt. Problems that cannot be solved nationally — climate change, pandemics, the loss of biodiversity, space research, global data flows, migration — are treated as a shared task of humanity and are financed, researched, and tackled cooperatively, with the knowledge gained shared openly rather than kept within closed elites. To make this work, SOLIS relies on concrete mechanisms: open, transparent trading platforms on which contracts and results can be inspected and conflicts of interest are disclosed; global ethics councils for economic questions that do not merely advise on large projects and agreements but oversee them; and multilateral innovation funds to which all contribute according to their economic strength and whose results are shared worldwide. The aim is neither self-sufficiency and isolation nor a world dictatorship, but a networked world economy in which cooperation is stronger than competition, rules apply to everyone and not only to the weak, and innovation is understood as a shared treasure. The world is too tightly interwoven and too vulnerable to carry on with the old patterns; the great challenges can only be met together.

Capital Allocation as a Network

This is where a question of power lies hidden that no economic order may overlook. The value point is pure circulation: it flows, but it does not accumulate. This banishes accumulation as a source of private supremacy — yet accumulation has not vanished; it has moved. Great undertakings require great resources, and the decision about which undertaking receives them is the most consequential economic decision of all. That decision now lies with the funds. So that no new summit arises here, the allocation of capital is not placed in one pair of hands but spread across a network: many funds side by side — regional, sectoral, and citizen-led — each built on the model of the order of power, partly chosen by lot, rotating, and accountable. What matters is that there are many routes: anyone who wants to realize an idea can turn to several independent funds, so that a no is never the system’s no, but only that of one door. A fixed share of the resources is always reserved for dissenters who challenge the prevailing consensus. In this way, the force that builds the future stays distributed and does not become a throne.

Saving without Domination

That value points cannot be hoarded is aimed at oligarchy, not at provision for the future. There is a difference, which the order respects, between accumulation that turns into power over others and moderate saving for one’s own plans. Value points therefore remain available for personal purposes over appropriate periods; their ceiling and expiry take effect only above the threshold at which savings would become a lever over other people’s lives. The medium thus prevents domination without taking away the individual’s ability to plan.

Work and Contribution

In an economy where machines and artificial intelligence take over more and more of the necessary work, an old question arises anew: what do people work for, and by what measure is a life successful? SOLIS separates contribution from compulsion. The foundations of life are secured without having to be earned; work is thus no longer the way to escape the struggle for survival, but a way of contributing something and finding oneself in what one does. What automation takes away in toil, it gives back in time — for care, education, creation, and community. And the measure of success shifts: the successful person is not the richest, but the one who creates something lasting for the community. The dignity of work depends not on what it yields, but on what it means.
