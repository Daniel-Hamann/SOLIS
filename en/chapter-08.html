Chapter 08

Health and Well-being

When Mara’s father received the diagnosis, there was no bill, no waiting list, and no question of whether it was still worth it. There was a doctor who read the machine’s suggestion, explained it, and disagreed with it. There was a room in the house that Jonas set up with a care team, and credit for the hours the family spent at the bedside. And there was, in the end, a farewell without pain, at home, with the river outside the window.

Imagine a world in which no one is afraid of falling ill, because they know: I will be cared for, I will be taken seriously, and I am not alone. In most of today’s systems, by contrast, health is a risk and a cost factor, a playing field between insurers, industry, and politics. Whoever has money receives treatment. Whoever has little receives rationing and waiting lists. Whoever is unlucky receives nothing in the end. SOLIS breaks with this logic. Health is not a market product but a right, a non-negotiable, non-commercial human right, and, as the economic order lays down, it belongs to the entitlements to which everyone is entitled without anything in return.

Health Care for All

Access is equal and of high quality for all, and it is meant to be humane and holistic. Holistic care does not only treat symptoms but understands their causes. It sees not only the body but also the soul, the mind, and the environment. A worker with chronic back pain therefore receives not only a painkiller but access to physiotherapy, work-related prevention, and psychological support, because health is more than merely functioning. From the start, this care includes prevention, mental health, and social support, because health depends not only on genes and lifestyle but also on security, community, and meaning. The health system therefore works together with social work, education, and neighbourhood networks. A young mother with postnatal depression receives, alongside medical help, a network of parents, counselling, and support, and she is seen as a whole person, not as a case.

All this is funded from the community share, which secures fairness and stability. Basic medical care is carried in solidarity, administered transparently, and knows no two-tier medicine and no fear for one’s existence because of a bill. Doctors, nurses, and therapists work in teams. Care reaches town and country equally, through local centres, mobile practices, and telemedicine, and people have a say when decisions are made about resources, priorities, and new procedures. Enterprises that offer optional services may exist, but they may never undermine the basic system or create privileged parallel worlds, because the market ends where life begins. Thus health becomes a common good and an expression of the ethical condition a society is in.

Prevention instead of Repair

In most of today’s health systems, the same pattern repeats itself. People live in unhealthy conditions and fall ill, and only then does the system react, late, expensively, and after the fact. It is like a fire brigade that arrives only when the house is already burning, instead of making sure that houses are built safely. SOLIS understands health not as a repair shop but as an active, forward-looking process that starts where illness arises. Prevention means more than the appeal to behave more healthily. It means shaping the entire environment so that health becomes the normal case, and recognizing problems early, before small ones become big. A young person who begins to isolate himself finds connection early, before depressive patterns take hold. An employee under heavy strain is offered other arrangements in good time, before she burns out.

Health is thus a cross-cutting task that is not offloaded onto the individual but carried by society. Urban planning creates green spaces, clean air, and safe routes. The world of work ensures healthy hours and a say for employees. Food systems make the healthy choice the affordable standard, education teaches from an early age what strengthens body and mind, and leisure activities are easy to reach and open to all. Thus prevention becomes a natural part of everyday life, from uncomplicated check-ups through easily accessible advice to offers of exercise and nutrition that suit people instead of patronizing them. Such a town offers exercise as joy and not as stress, healthy food in schools and enterprises, and time for rest, and enterprises gain healthy, motivated people instead of worn-out workers. Prevention saves not only resources, it saves suffering, and it makes a system more agile and more durable, because it recognizes developments early instead of chasing after catastrophes.

Mental Health

In many societies, mental health is considered a private matter, a flaw about which one had better stay silent, with the result of stigmatization, shame, isolation, and overcrowded waiting lists. SOLIS reverses this picture. Mental health is as central as physical health, it is a social and not only an individual matter, and it is not attended to only when someone breaks down but considered and cared for from the start.

Every person has a right to psychological support, without bureaucratic hurdles, without ruinous costs, without months of waiting, and without the fear of stigma. This includes counselling and care services everywhere, crisis help that can be reached reliably, and tiered systems of help that range from preventive counselling to intensive therapy. A young woman with the first symptoms of anxiety finds access immediately and not only after months. Mental health, moreover, is not a matter for clinics alone but a cross-cutting concern that is considered everywhere. In schools this happens through programmes for resilience, mindfulness, and an open climate of conversation, and through people pupils can confide in. In the world of work it happens through compulsory prevention of overwork, flexible hours, and managers trained in their responsibility for the mental health of their people. In communities it happens through neighbourhood centres and easily accessible points of contact.

This requires a social climate of openness in which one may say that one is not doing well and, instead of being rebuffed with the demand to pull oneself together, finds a hearing. For many of today’s burdens, exhaustion, depression, loneliness, and anxiety, are not individual weaknesses but consequences of the system: of overload, isolation, and loss of meaning. An order that recognizes this and creates structures for prevention and healing gives people permission to be human and at the same time spares itself the high follow-on costs of suppressed suffering.

Social Health and Community

When people talk about health, they think of the body and perhaps of the soul. There is, however, a third, often invisible dimension: social health, that is, the question of how connected a person is with others. The research here is clear. Loneliness harms health to a degree comparable to smoking, and people who are socially connected live longer and more healthily. Community is not a pleasant extra but a basic biological, psychological, and cultural need, and SOLIS makes it a task of the system.

In SOLIS, community does not arise by chance but is consciously shaped and protected. Architecture, urban planning, and public spaces are laid out so that encounter and mutual support come easily. Clubs, networks, and easily accessible opportunities for exchange receive space and support, and places such as parks, libraries, and community centres remain open and are not taken over by commerce. Loneliness is not considered private misfortune but a social problem that is tackled deliberately: by paying attention to where isolation arises, for instance among old people, single parents, or newcomers, and by programmes that create connection. An older woman whose children live far away thus remains part of a network across the generations, in which she contributes her experience and at the same time finds support.

Linked to this is a different understanding of strength. Where many societies today teach that everyone should look out for themselves and show no weakness, SOLIS holds that real strength grows from connection, compassion, and cooperation. Children therefore learn not only specialist knowledge but how to deal with feelings, how to work in a team, and how to take on responsibility. Care is not devalued as a lesser activity but highly esteemed and paid as a contribution. A society that fosters community is more stable, more peaceful, and more creative, because people catch one another when they fall. And like every good order, it measures itself by how it treats the weakest, not by how brightly the strongest shine.

Digital Health Systems

The digitization of medicine brings hope and fear at the same time: the hope of better diagnoses and easier access, the fear of misuse of data and cold automation. SOLIS takes a clear path here that follows the principles of the whole order. Technology is a tool in the service of people, not a ruler over them. It extends what people can do but never replaces what only people can do: compassion, presence, and relationship.

Every person has a digital health record that brings together all essential information, is securely encrypted and stored in a decentralized way, and is made accessible only with their consent. In an emergency, rescue workers have limited access to the relevant data without laying bare the whole profile. Control over the data thus lies, as the order generally requires, with the person themselves and not with a private exploiter. Artificial intelligence supports the system at many levels, in evaluating findings, in the early detection of rare diseases, and in personal recommendations, but it is never the deciding authority. The rules that the chapter on technology lays down for every artificial intelligence apply. Human oversight is compulsory, decisions are not blindly automated, and patients have the right to understand a recommendation, to question it, and to reject it. A doctor examines the suggestion, discusses it, and explains the alternatives instead of simply following it. Telemedicine, mobile services, and wearable devices overcome distances, so that people in remote regions also gain access to modern care and a chronically ill person receives help in time through a connected device. In all this, however, a limit applies. Technology can explain, monitor, and improve, but it cannot hold a hand, cannot comfort, and cannot understand what it means to be human. Digital systems create time, reach, and precision, but the heart of healing and care remains the human relationship.

Global Health Networks

There is a contradiction in today’s world: diseases know no borders, but health systems very much do. When a virus breaks out, an environmental toxin is released, or a biotechnological danger looms, nations often react with isolation, blame, and the logic of national advantage, and this costs time, lives, and trust. SOLIS holds that health is always global, because what happens anywhere affects everyone, and it builds structures that work across nations and beyond the profit motive.

These include internationally connected early warning systems that detect medical, ecological, and technical signals early and share them openly, supported by local laboratories and sensors, by analyses that are always accompanied by human review, and by crisis teams that act quickly and in a coordinated way. If a new kind of fever appears in a remote region, it is neither covered up nor waited out. A coordinated response of analysis, support, and action takes effect. Medical breakthroughs are not a geopolitical toy. Research results are shared instead of hidden, medicines and vaccines are distributed according to need instead of market power, and the recognition of inventors never comes at the expense of justice. A vaccine against a dangerous virus is therefore made available deliberately where it is needed most urgently.

That wealthy regions support the building of robust health systems in poorer ones is, in this order, not charity but rational necessity. A virus that rages uncontrolled somewhere spreads everywhere, and a weak system endangers not only the people on the spot but the whole of humanity. Investment in infrastructure, training, and decentralized production therefore serves the security of supply for everyone. Thus every person has a better chance of staying healthy, regardless of where they were born, and the competition between states becomes a shared project: a community of fate that shares its knowledge and its means because that is only natural.

Old Age and Farewell

An order that takes people seriously accompanies them across the whole arc of their lives, up to the end. In SOLIS, old age is not a time of being shunted aside but a full stage of life. Older people remain part of the community, their experience is needed, in the assemblies, in the schools, and as mentors of the young, and their care is a respected task that society carries together, not a burden to be hidden. Where a person depends on care, they receive it without losing their dignity, and whoever gives it receives recognition and credit for it. And because life includes its end, SOLIS gives dying a humane framework. No one should have to die alone, in avoidable pain, or degraded. Accompaniment at the end of life, the relief of suffering, and respect for the will of the dying belong to a health order that not only keeps people alive but lets them go with dignity.
