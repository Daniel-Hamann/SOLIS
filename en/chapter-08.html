Chapter 08

Health and Well-being

Health Care for All

In SOLIS, health is not a market product but a right — a non-negotiable, non-commercial human right. In most of today’s systems, by contrast, it is a risk and a cost factor, a playing field for insurers, industry, and politics: those with money get treatment; those with little get rationing and waiting lists; and those who are unlucky end up with nothing. SOLIS breaks with this logic. As set out in the economic order, health is one of the entitlements due to everyone, with nothing required in return.

Access is equal and of high quality for everyone, and it is meant to be humane and holistic. Holistic care does not just treat symptoms but seeks to understand their causes; it looks not only at the body but also at the psyche, the mind, and a person’s surroundings. A worker with chronic back pain therefore receives not just a painkiller but access to physiotherapy, workplace prevention, and psychological support, because health is more than simply functioning. From the outset, this care includes prevention, mental health, and social support, because health depends not only on genes and lifestyle but also on security, community, and meaning; the health system therefore works hand in hand with social work, education, and neighbourhood networks.

All this is carried by public funding that ensures fairness and stability. Basic medical care is funded collectively and administered transparently, and it knows no two-tier medicine and no existential fear of a doctor’s bill. Private providers may exist, for example for elective treatments or special services, but they may never undermine the basic system or create privileged parallel worlds, because the market ends where life begins. Health thus becomes a shared good and a reflection of a society’s ethical condition.

Prevention instead of Repair

In most of today’s health systems, the same pattern repeats itself: people live in unhealthy conditions, fall ill, and only then does the system react — late, expensively, and after the fact. It is like a fire brigade that turns out only once the house is already on fire, instead of making sure houses are built safely in the first place. SOLIS understands health not as a repair shop, but as an active, forward-looking process that starts where illness begins. Prevention here means more than urging people to live more healthily; it means shaping the entire environment so that health becomes the norm, and spotting problems early, before small ones become big ones. A young person who begins to withdraw finds connection early, before depressive patterns take hold; an employee under heavy strain is offered flexible arrangements in time, before she burns out. Health thus becomes a cross-cutting task that is not offloaded onto individuals but carried by society: urban planning creates green spaces, clean air, and safe routes; the world of work ensures healthy working hours and a say for employees; food systems make the healthy choice the affordable default; education teaches from an early age what strengthens body and mind; and leisure activities are low-threshold and open to all. Prevention thus becomes a natural part of everyday life — from straightforward check-ups and easily accessible counselling to exercise and nutrition programmes that suit people rather than lecturing them. A city like this makes movement a pleasure rather than a chore, offers healthy food in schools and workplaces, and leaves time for recovery; and companies benefit from healthy, motivated people instead of worn-out staff. Prevention saves not only money but suffering, and it makes a system more flexible and more sustainable, because it spots trends early instead of chasing after disasters.

Mental Health

In many societies, mental health is considered a private matter, a stain better kept quiet — with the result of stigma, shame, isolation, and overflowing waiting lists. SOLIS turns this picture around: mental health is as central as physical health; it is a matter for society, not just for the individual; and it is not attended to only when someone breaks down, but considered and cared for from the start.

Everyone has a right to psychological support — without bureaucratic hurdles, ruinous costs, or months of waiting, and without fear of stigma. This includes comprehensive counselling and care, reliably available crisis support, and tiered support systems ranging from preventive counselling to intensive therapy, so that a young woman with early symptoms of anxiety gets help straight away and not only after months. Mental health is also not a matter for clinics alone, but a cross-cutting concern that is considered everywhere: in schools through programmes for resilience, mindfulness, and an open culture of conversation, as well as through trusted contact persons; in the world of work through mandatory stress prevention, flexible hours, and managers trained in their psychological responsibility; and in communities through neighbourhood centres and low-threshold points of contact.

All this requires a social climate of openness, in which people may say that they are not doing well and are not told to pull themselves together, but are listened to. For many of today’s burdens — exhaustion, depression, loneliness, anxiety — are not individual weaknesses but systemic consequences of overload, isolation, and loss of meaning. An order that recognizes this and creates both preventive and healing structures gives people permission to be human, and at the same time saves the high costs that suppressed suffering brings later on.

Social Health and Community

When people talk about health, they think of the body and perhaps of the mind; yet there is a third, often invisible dimension: social health — how connected a person is with others. The research is unequivocal: loneliness is as harmful as smoking, and people who are socially connected live longer and healthier lives. Community is not a pleasant extra but a basic biological, psychological, and cultural need, and SOLIS makes it a task of the system.

In SOLIS, community does not arise by chance but is deliberately shaped and protected. Architecture, urban planning, and public spaces are designed to make encounters and mutual support easy; clubs, networks, and low-threshold opportunities to meet are given space and support; and places such as parks, libraries, and community centres remain open and are not taken over by commerce. Loneliness counts not as a private misfortune but as a social problem that is tackled deliberately — by paying attention to where isolation arises, for instance among older people, single parents, or newcomers, and through programmes that foster connection. An older woman whose children live far away thus remains part of a web across generations, in which she contributes her experience and at the same time finds support.

This goes hand in hand with a different understanding of strength. Where many of today’s societies teach that everyone should look out for themselves and never show weakness, SOLIS holds that true strength grows out of connection, compassion, and cooperation. Children therefore learn not only subject knowledge but emotional intelligence, teamwork, and how to take responsibility, and care work is not looked down on as inferior but held in high regard. A society that fosters community is more stable, more peaceful, and more creative, because people catch one another when they fall — and like every good order, it measures itself by how it treats the weakest, not by how brightly the strongest shine.

Digital Health Systems

The digitalization of medicine brings hope and fear at the same time: the hope of better diagnoses and easier access, the fear of data misuse and cold automation. SOLIS takes a clear path here that follows the principles of the whole order: technology is a tool in the service of human beings, not their master. It extends what people can do, but never replaces what only people can do — compassion, presence, and relationship.

Everyone has a digital health record that brings together all essential information, is securely encrypted and stored in a decentralized way, and can be accessed only with the person’s consent; in an emergency, paramedics have limited access to the relevant data without seeing the entire profile. Control over the data thus lies, as the order demands throughout, with the person themselves and not with a private company exploiting it. Artificial intelligence supports the system at many levels — in evaluating test results, detecting rare diseases early, and making personalized recommendations — yet it is never the deciding authority. Human oversight is mandatory, decisions are not blindly automated, and patients have the right to understand, question, and reject a recommendation; a doctor reviews an AI’s suggestion, discusses it, and explains the alternatives instead of simply following it. Telemedicine, mobile services, and wearable devices overcome geographical and financial barriers, so that people in remote regions also have access to modern care, and a chronically ill person, for instance, gets timely help through a connected device. In all of this, however, one limit holds: technology can explain, monitor, and optimize, but it cannot hold a hand, cannot comfort, and cannot understand what it means to be human. Digital systems are tools, not substitutes for people; they create time, reach, and precision, yet the heart of healing and care remains the human relationship.

Global Health Networks

There is a contradiction in today’s world: diseases know no borders, but health systems very much do. When a virus breaks out, an environmental toxin is released, or a biotechnological danger looms, nations often react by closing themselves off, assigning blame, and pursuing national advantage — and that costs time, lives, and trust. SOLIS holds that health is always global, because what happens anywhere concerns everyone, and it builds structures that work across nations and beyond the profit motive.

These include internationally connected early-warning systems that pick up medical, ecological, and technological signals early and share them transparently, supported by local laboratories and sensors, by analyses that are always accompanied by human review, and by global crisis teams that act quickly and in coordination. When a new fever appears in a remote region, it is neither covered up nor left to run its course; instead, a coordinated response of analysis, support, and action begins. Medical breakthroughs are not a geopolitical plaything here: research results are shared rather than hidden, medicines and vaccines are distributed according to need rather than market power, and patents protect innovation, but not at the expense of global health equity. A vaccine against a dangerous virus is therefore made available precisely where it is needed most.

In this order, it is not charity but rational necessity for wealthy countries to support the development of robust health systems in poorer ones, because a virus that spreads unchecked in one place spreads everywhere, and a weak system endangers not only the people there but all of humanity. Investment in local infrastructure, training, and decentralized production therefore secures supply for everyone. Every person thus has a better chance of staying healthy, wherever they were born, and the competition between states gives way to a common project — a community of shared fate that shares its knowledge and its resources as a matter of course.

The Whole of Life: Old Age and Farewell

An order that takes human beings seriously accompanies them across the whole arc of their lives, right to the end. In SOLIS, old age is not a siding but a full stage of life: older people remain part of the community, their experience is needed, and caring for them is an honoured task that society carries together, not a burden to be hidden away. Those who depend on care receive it without losing their dignity. And because the end is part of life too, SOLIS gives dying a humane setting: no one should have to die alone, in avoidable pain, or in degrading conditions. Support at the end of life, the relief of suffering, and respect for the wishes of the dying belong to a health order that does not merely keep people alive, but lets them go with dignity.
