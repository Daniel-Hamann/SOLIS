Chapter 06

Planet, Ecology, and Regeneration

Of the five foundations, responsibility for the planet is one of the load-bearing pillars, and yet until now it has had no chapter of its own; it was scattered across the economy, technology, and everyday life. This chapter brings it together. For an order that aims to carry humanity beyond this generation depends on a condition that no majority can suspend: a living planet. On a ruined Earth, the question of the best order no longer arises.

The Planetary Boundaries

The starting point is an insight that comes before politics because it comes from physics: the planet has limits. Science calls them the planetary boundaries — the stability of the climate, the diversity of life, the purity of water, air, and soil — and beyond these limits the system that sustains all life tips over. SOLIS therefore treats them as what they are: a wall within which all economic activity and all freedom have their space. Ecological costs are built into every price by rule, so that doing harm costs more than sparing; the limits of extraction are not left to the market but set and monitored. Once something has been recognized as a limit of survival, no boom can lift it again.

Regeneration, Not Mere Preservation

Where too much has already been destroyed, protection is no longer enough. SOLIS therefore goes beyond preservation to restoration: not only doing less harm, but actively healing. Forests are not only kept but brought back; soils are not only spared but built up; waters are not only polluted less but restored to life. The measure of success thus shifts from the least harm to the greatest recovery. A generation that gives the planet back more than it takes reverses the direction in which humanity has long treated the living world.

The Closed Cycle

Nature knows no waste; what one creature excretes is another’s food. SOLIS makes this principle the basic form of its economy. The straight line from extraction through use to rubbish is replaced by the closed cycle: products are designed so that their materials become raw materials again, repair comes before replacement, and reuse before consumption. Yet this circle does not merely go round and round. It keeps materials flowing and at the same time carries knowledge and prosperity upward. A cycle in matter, progress in the mind — that is the spiral in which a civilization can endure without consuming itself.

The Rights of the Living World

SOLIS widens the circle of concern beyond human beings. An ecosystem, a river, a forest are not mere property and raw material; they carry a weight of their own that counts in decisions. Where far-reaching projects affect the living world, it is given a voice through appointed stewards who represent its interests, just as a guardian represents the interests of someone who cannot speak for themselves. And because the planet also belongs to future generations, every act of extraction is subject to the proviso that those who come after will still find it habitable. The ethics councils therefore explicitly examine projects against this twofold claim: that of the living world and that of the people still to come.

Energy and the Long Future

The material basis of a lasting civilization is energy that does not consume the planet. SOLIS therefore relies on renewable, decentralized, and resilient energy systems — not only because they protect the climate, but because a distributed supply can absorb failures that would bring down a centralized one. If one part fails, the others carry the load; no single node holds society’s supply in its hands. Here ecological necessity meets the logic of the whole work: no monopoly over a central resource, but a network whose strength lies in its distribution. Only on this basis — a recovering planet and energy that does not devour it — does the distant future this work speaks of become possible at all.
