Chapter 12

Implementation and Transition

The Path from What Exists to SOLIS

An order like SOLIS cannot be switched on overnight. A society cannot be reprogrammed in a day, fears and obstacles cannot simply be argued away, and — this is crucial — such an order cannot be imposed by force either, without betraying its own foundations. An order built on dignity and maturity cannot be installed through a coup; it has to be won through persuasion and through successful example. SOLIS is therefore a long, deliberate process of transformation that takes time, patience, courage, and a culture of learning from mistakes. This path runs through three phases that interlock. It begins with a change of consciousness, because before structures change, minds and hearts have to move: honest assessments that show not only problems but also possibilities, and courageous voices in the media, art, science, and everyday life who dare to ask whether we really want to carry on like this. Next comes the building of beacons — concrete places and projects that show it can be done: cities that switch to a circular economy, companies that work according to these principles, schools and research networks that try out new approaches. Such examples inspire, prove that change is practice and not just theory, and build momentum. Only when enough people are convinced and enough examples hold up does systemic change begin — new legal frameworks, new economic incentives, new institutions — and this too happens not overnight but step by step, with setbacks, accompanied by monitoring and correction. The transition to SOLIS is therefore a project spanning generations, perhaps the greatest humanity has ever undertaken.

An Honest Assessment

Two things need to be said clearly here. First, this transition comes with no guarantee, and it will meet not only inertia but real resistance. Second, and harder: SOLIS redistributes power and wealth, and those who hold both in concentrated form under the existing order — together with the institutions built to protect them — will not be moved by arguments, because what is at stake for them is not understanding but interest. A design that assumes the powerful can be persuaded is naive, and SOLIS makes no such assumption. It renounces coercion, but not realism — and how it deals with this resistance without betraying itself is the subject of the section on actors and resistance.

Stages of the Transformation

The transformation to SOLIS is not a sudden upheaval but a relay race in which the stages build on and reinforce one another. The order is not arbitrary; it follows an inner logic. It begins with education, because without the ability to think in complex terms, reflect ethically, and act creatively, every later reform comes to nothing. This education starts not at university but in early childhood, and it prepares people to shape change in the first place. Next comes the economy as the most powerful lever: incentives shift from short-term gain towards sustainable, socially and ecologically meaningful activity, and a new corporate culture — participation, ethical goals for innovation, responsibility towards society and the planet — takes the place of pure profit logic. Once the economy changes, governance must follow, so that political and institutional structures carry the change: more transparency, more ethical oversight, more participation, and, step by step, the bodies of the order of power described in this work take shape. In parallel, work is done on the soft factors — on values, stories, and how we understand success, happiness, and meaning — because the successful person in SOLIS is not the richest but the one who creates something lasting for the community. In the end, a local process becomes a global one that learns from itself, respects differences, and shares common principles.

For this to succeed, it needs strategic roadmaps that set out stages, priorities, and responsibilities; accompanying education and culture programmes that make people participants rather than mere bystanders; pilot projects with clear strategies for spreading what works; and global platforms for sharing knowledge and resources. Transformation thus becomes not chaos but a deliberate process carried by many.

Actors, Roles, and Resistance

The change to SOLIS does not happen by itself; it is carried by people. Three roles are central. The pioneers are the courageous first movers who try out new ideas, often against the current and at their own risk — social entrepreneurs, municipalities, teachers, and artists who live out what is at first only theory, and so build momentum. The bridge-builders are less visible and yet indispensable: they bring together what would otherwise stay apart — citizens and politics, science and business, young and old — translating between worlds and creating spaces in which people with different interests work on solutions together. Finally, the guardians watch over ethics and law: ethics councils, ombudspersons, and platforms for whistle-blowers ensure that the change does not proceed blindly or recklessly, protect the weaker, and hold SOLIS to its own standards.

Not All Resistance Is Alike

Wherever there is movement, there is resistance, but it is not all of one kind. Some resistance is a legitimate signal: it shows where people are afraid, where there are real conflicts of interest, or where someone has been overlooked. When a pilot project for sustainable urban development meets protest because rents are rising, that is no reason to abandon it but a sign that mechanisms for social balance are missing; such resistance is listened to and turned into better solutions. Other resistance, however, is not a signal for improvement but the defence of privilege by those who stand to lose power and wealth, and it does not dissolve through dialogue, because it does not rest on a misunderstanding. Treating both kinds alike is the naivety on which great designs have foundered. SOLIS distinguishes between them and meets each in its own way.

Building in Parallel instead of Asking Permission

Because the powerful will not give up their power, SOLIS does not wait for their consent. Its strategy is not to conquer the existing centres of power, but to build parallel structures that simply work better — cooperative enterprises, provision from common goods, deliberative bodies, open knowledge networks, and resilient, broadly based supply chains — and to let them grow until they overtake and absorb the old structures. The transition is thus won less through conquest than by making the old order obsolete: where the new order simply serves people better, the old one loses its grip. This is not a detour but the actual path, because it needs none of the coercion that SOLIS rejects, and it bases the new order on what it achieves rather than on what it conquers.

The Dangers, Named Plainly

This path can fail, and naming the dangers is itself a protection against them. The first is capture: the new institutions being taken over by the very interests they were meant to replace. The order of power, selection by lot, rotation, and the right of recall work against this by making lasting takeover mechanically difficult. The second is economic strangulation: an island of SOLIS, surrounded by a hostile market, being starved out or undercut. The controlled border membrane and the building of resilient, networked, and cooperative structures, rather than isolated experiments, work against this. The third is hollowing-out: the language of SOLIS being adopted while its substance is emptied. Measurable monitoring of fundamental rights and the maturity of citizens, which allows hollowing-out to be recognized, work against this. The fourth is relapse: the order eroding over time. Deep civic education and the structural impossibility of entrenchment work against this. A society that keeps these dangers in view is far harder to defeat by them.

No Coercion, but Defence

Finally, there is a distinction on which everything depends. SOLIS renounces attack and any attempt to establish itself by force; it is not installed through an overthrow and does not compel the unwilling. Yet renouncing coercion does not mean renouncing defence. A society that has built something just is not obliged to let it be destroyed, sabotaged, or hijacked in the name of tolerance. The line runs as in the chapter on security: attack is forbidden, defence is legitimate. SOLIS protects what it builds against those who want to tear it down — without itself becoming the aggressor it rejects.

Communication and Persuasion

No idea, however good, survives without communication, because people need to understand what it is about, feel that it concerns them, and see that they can take part. In SOLIS, communication is therefore not an add-on or a PR machine, but the heart of the transformation: it connects, explains, inspires, and mobilizes.

Above all, it explains not only what is happening but why. Too often today, reforms are pushed through and laws passed without people understanding the reasons, which breeds mistrust and withdrawal. SOLIS therefore always answers the questions of purpose and meaning as well: when a city switches to renewable energy, it communicates not only the technical details but also why the change is necessary, what it means in the long run, and what role each individual plays. This communication is aimed at everyone, not only at elites — at the factory worker, the young person, the pensioner, and the single parent as much as at the entrepreneur — in clear, respectful language without technocratic jargon, and it uses every channel, from traditional media and digital platforms to local events and the arts. Change in a city therefore does not begin with an administrative decree alone, but just as much with a festival of music, conversation, and shared experience.

Finally, what matters is that communication is a dialogue and does not run from the top down. The point is not to broadcast messages, but to create spaces in which questions can be asked, doubts voiced, and proposals made, and in which those responsible talk with the population rather than about it. A new law is therefore not only explained; there is also an open phase in which citizens ask questions, help shape it, and raise concerns. The result is a society that does not merely endure change but carries it together.

International Spread and Adaptation

SOLIS is not a finished template to be laid over the world, because every society has its own history, its own symbols, and its own resources, strengths, and wounds. It therefore sees itself not as an export product but as an open, adaptable model: it offers core principles and an ethical framework, while the concrete form emerges locally.

The core principles are the same everywhere. Human dignity, sustainability, justice, participation, and innovation that serves the common good apply in Brazil as in Finland, in Ghana as in Japan; they form the ethical backbone that is not up for negotiation. What differs is the how. Which institutions are built, which paths are chosen, and which symbols and rituals carry the system depend on each society: in an Indigenous community, protecting traditional knowledge systems comes first; in a post-industrial metropolis, social and technical innovation; in a region scarred by war, building resilience before complex structures of governance can emerge. Every society thus fills the framework with its own reality, instead of having a foreign model imposed on it.

So that SOLIS does not break up into isolated islands, international networks connect the individual paths. Through them, knowledge is shared — which approaches have worked, where there were setbacks, and what can be learned from them; resources are distributed so that poorer regions receive the same opportunities; and standards are coordinated, so that basic ecological and human-rights principles apply everywhere, even where they are implemented differently. A network of SOLIS communities on every continent exchanges ideas, runs joint projects, and shares experience without flattening local differences. For the great challenges — climate, pandemics, the loss of biodiversity, migration — can only be met together, and the diversity of paths is not a weakness here but a source of innovation and resilience. SOLIS therefore seeks neither self-sufficiency nor isolation, nor a world dictatorship, but a humanity that is united and at the same time diverse.
