Chapter 09

Culture and Meaning

Culture as Living Heritage

In a world that is becoming ever faster and more technical, something is in danger of being lost that lies at the very core of being human: culture. It is not mere entertainment or decoration, but the memory and inner compass of a society — what sustains people, tells them who they are and where they come from, and builds bridges between past, present, and future. SOLIS therefore recognizes culture as a fundamental dimension, not a side issue.

Every cultural heritage counts as valuable here, whether great or small, global or local. There are no important and unimportant cultures, no great and small narratives; an Indigenous story carries as much weight as a classical work, a regional dialect as much as a world language. Cultures may change, grow, and mix, and at the same time protect what is sacred to them: a community may decide for itself which of its traditions it shares and which it keeps, rather than surrendering them to outside access.

So that culture is not left to the market or to chance, there are active programmes for preserving, documenting, and nurturing it — archives that safeguard languages, stories, and works; support for those who create and pass on culture; and spaces in which cultural knowledge stays alive. A programme that brings younger and older people together, so that the older generation can share its stories and knowledge, preserves culture not as nostalgia but as living community. For people need roots to find their bearings, and societies need culture to foster cohesion and to resist uprooting. Diversity here is not a problem but a source of richness.

Art, Music, and Literature

In many of today’s societies, art has become a luxury good, a market commodity, or a prestige project. Yet it is far more: the language of the soul, the tool through which a society looks at itself, and a laboratory for new ideas and feelings. It links the individual and the community, reality and vision. SOLIS therefore makes art, music, and literature a load-bearing pillar.

Artistic creation is encouraged, not merely tolerated. Public programmes support artists in every discipline, regardless of market value and mass taste, and the experimental and the critical in particular are given room; a theatre group developing a play about social inequality is supported because it matters to society, not because it sells. Art here is not a spectator sport: children and adults alike are encouraged to create for themselves — to paint, make music, write, and act — without pressure to perform and without elitist hurdles, and older people bring in their stories, from which younger people learn.

Art is judged not by its market value, but by its expressive power, its sincerity, and the impulse it gives to society. Street art, digital art, and traditional forms are taken equally seriously, and those who take up social or ecological questions receive particular support. A young person who finds their form of expression in graffiti therefore gets not just a criminal charge, but walls, workshops, and guidance to make something of their energy. For art gives individuals a voice and works for society like an immune system: it senses where something is ailing, makes visible what lies beneath the surface, and builds bridges where words fail.

Spirituality and Philosophy

In many modern societies, the search for meaning is considered a private matter, religion something that does not belong in public space, and philosophy the business of a few specialists. Yet human beings need more than food, housing, and technology; they need meaning, depth, and a sense of connection to the whole, and they need spaces in which to ask the big questions — who they are, what life means, and what we owe one another. SOLIS recognizes this dimension as fundamental and makes it part of the social order. Every form of spirituality is respected, as long as it does no harm to others. Religion, non-belief, and every other form of the search for meaning stand side by side as equals; no one is forced to believe anything, and no one may impose their beliefs on others. A religious woman can practise her rituals as freely as a monk meditates or a thinker argues in philosophical conversation, and the protection of this freedom ends only where discrimination or coercion begins. To this end there are public spaces for silence and reflection, open to everyone, as well as shared celebrations and days of remembrance that are tied to no religion but honour our common humanity.

Philosophy, finally, is not an elite project but a tool for everyone. Children learn early on to reflect on the good life, on justice, and on happiness, and adults find spaces for shared inquiry in which the point is not the one right answer but thinking itself. A society that allows such questions is more resilient and more empathetic, because it meets existential uncertainty with depth rather than distraction. This ties in directly with the maturity grounded in the chapter on education: meaning cannot be decreed, only found, and an order that takes this seriously invites reflection instead of steering it.

Rituals and Shared Experiences

In the modern world, much has become purely functional — work, consumption, even leisure — and there is often little room for shared experience, for moments that step outside the flow of everyday life. SOLIS recognizes this as a basic human need. Rituals are not mere folklore or religious tradition, but bridges: between people and generations, between human beings and nature, between the individual and the community.

In SOLIS, rituals are not prescribed but are free to develop. They grow out of people’s needs and experiences and may change and reinvent themselves; there is room for traditional forms as well as new ones, such as ecological or artistic rituals. A city develops a festival together with its citizens, in which art, music, nature, and community come together — not as an attraction for outsiders but as an expression of a living identity. Such rituals can be religious or cultural, social or ecological, and they create moments of pause, connection, and meaning.

In a world that is often restless and fragmented, they are the moments that remind us that we belong together and share something greater. They are not just a programme but meaning that is lived, and they strengthen the community because they show that it is more than the sum of its parts. To make this work, communities receive the means to cultivate and develop their own rituals, and participation remains open to all, regardless of background, belief, or status.

Creating Meaning and Personal Fulfilment

In many modern societies, work counts as a way of earning money, success as career and possessions, and relationships as a private matter. In all this, a crucial question goes unanswered: why do we do what we do, and what truly fulfils us? SOLIS puts this question at the centre, because a good society is not only efficient and prosperous, but a space in which people can find meaning — not prescribed and not uniform, but personal and alive. In SOLIS, it goes without saying that people may ask themselves what is meaningful to them, where they want to make a difference, and how they can be part of something greater without losing themselves. Children are encouraged to be curious and to try things out; young people are supported in discovering their calling, guided not by income alone but by passion and contribution; and adults have the right to change direction when their path no longer fits, so that an engineer who longs to do social work can retrain without fear of losing status. Life here is not divided into useful and superfluous areas: work should not only function but provide meaning, and a contribution such as volunteering with children is valued, even when it does not primarily create economic value.

To this end, there are services that help people find their own sense of purpose — counselling, mentoring, and spaces for reflection; communities that support people through transitions; and an education that includes not only subject knowledge but personal development. This is more than a soft concern: a person without meaning becomes empty and alienated inside, and a society full of alienated people is sick, however brightly its façade may shine. Every person has a right to a life worth living — not merely to survive, but to live.

Global Cultural Exchange

The present is marked by two opposing movements: on one side, cultures are becoming ever more interconnected; on the other, fears of appropriation, loss of identity, and cultural swamping are growing. This breeds uncertainty and mistrust, and living exchange often gives way to withdrawal and recrimination. SOLIS takes a different path and understands global cultural exchange not as a superficial ornament but as a necessity in a tightly interwoven world.

Exchange is therefore actively encouraged — through partnerships between schools and cultural centres, through collaboration between artists across borders, and through festivals and platforms that connect not only elites but also local initiatives. A music festival does not simply put people from different continents on stage one after another, but lets them create something together that none of them could have invented alone. The question of cultural appropriation is neither condemned wholesale nor carelessly brushed aside, but handled with awareness and dialogue: where does a form come from, what does it mean, and who has access to it? Marginalized groups can protect their forms of expression, and cultures may share what they wish to share and draw boundaries where they wish. Anyone who wants to work with the patterns of a community therefore enters into an exchange with it as a partner, rather than simply copying it.

In this way, diversity is understood as a treasure, not a threat. People discover new perspectives and forms of expression, which broadens horizons and strengthens empathy; societies that live exchange are more resilient and more creative; and in an interconnected world, the great challenges can only be met together. Instead of barricading themselves behind walls, people come together in open squares and let art, music, and ideas flow — as the shared treasure of humanity.

Culture and Identity

Culture gives meaning, creates identity, and connects generations, yet it can also divide when it is co-opted, and serve propaganda when it submits to power or the market. In many of today’s systems it becomes either a commodity or a tool of ideology, and loses its free, connecting character in the process. SOLIS understands culture as free, diverse, and at the same time shared — as an expression of individual creativity and a foundation of social cohesion. The first consequence is the freedom of culture. Art, language, religion, and tradition are freely chosen; no one is forced to give up their cultural identity; and culture may be critical and challenge authority — including the order itself, in keeping with the maturity grounded in the chapter on education. An artist may therefore create political art without fear of censorship or commercial pressure, and a community may cultivate its traditions, as long as it respects the immovable threshold of fundamental rights. So that culture belongs to everyone and not to a few, it remains a common good: museums, libraries, theatres, and digital archives are open to all, education carries diversity forward, and cultural work is supported by funds rather than subjected to market pressure, so that a young musician can find support without becoming dependent on commercial structures.

Finally, identity in SOLIS is multidimensional and fluid, not one-dimensional and decreed. A person can see themselves as part of their region, their profession, and the world all at once, without these levels coming into conflict, and identity may grow rather than being fixed from above. Culture thus becomes a bridge that builds trust and shared values, enables dialogue between generations and groups, and offers orientation especially in times of change. Diversity is a strength here, as long as it is not artificially misused to divide — culture is free and not for sale; identity grows and is not decreed.
