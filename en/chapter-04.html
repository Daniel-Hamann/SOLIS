Chapter 04

Security, Defence, and Peace

On the night the river rose, the same sign lit up on every screen in the town: heightened alert, declared at ten past eleven, justified in three sentences, valid until Wednesday. Mara stood on the dyke with the Disaster Corps. Jonas, her husband, was carrying beds up to the top floor of the care home with his team. On Wednesday the water level fell, and the alert expired without anyone having to end it. Whatever powers the night had needed were gone again by Thursday, and a panel chosen by lot read the report.

In the end, every order rests on a question that many designs avoid: who controls force? Since Max Weber, the state has been defined as the entity that claims the monopoly of legitimate force. Whoever commands this monopoly, over the police, defence, and ultimate coercion, ultimately rules everything else, however carefully the other bodies are arranged. A Ring that distributes power but leaves force undivided would be a mere façade. Hence the principle of this chapter: security is not the exception to the order, but its strictest application.

Who Holds the Sword

The temptation is to create, in the name of security, an authority that stands above the rules: in an emergency, to protect everyone, for a limited time. Historically, almost every freedom has died at this point, because emergency powers, once granted, are rarely given back. The power that was meant to weather the storm outlasts the storm. SOLIS concludes from this that there may be no place where force escapes control. The answer to the old question of who guards the guardians is, here too, not a higher guard. It is the same as everywhere else: a structure in which no one can entrench themselves.

The Ringed Apparatus of Force

Four components bind force, just as four components bind political power. First, civilian oversight by bodies filled partly by lot: the armed bodies are watched not by their own kind but by the citizenry, and the leadership of the security forces stands under the assembly, not beside it. Second, the rotation of command and the time limits on office, so that no lasting cartel and no personal following forms around force. Third, the separation of command from execution and of ordering from review: whoever orders force may not at the same time rule on its lawfulness. Fourth, the complete traceability of every operation, every order, and every procurement. Weapons and equipment are procured in open procedures with open books, without hidden influence, and nothing that serves force comes into being in the dark.

Above these components stands an immovable limit: the force of the order may never be turned inwards against political dissent. Just as the intervention procedure of the media order may never be turned against criticism of SOLIS, the security apparatus may never be turned against those who oppose the order. This limit prevents the protection of the order from becoming the tool of its ossification.

Internal Security without a Police State

In SOLIS, security at home does not come first from surveillance and deterrence, but from removing the causes of crime. Where no one is pushed into crime by need, exclusion, or a lack of prospects, crime falls before any patrol goes looking for it. Where protection is nevertheless needed, the principle of the mildest means applies: as little coercion as possible, and force only as a response to force. The security forces are part of the community, not its occupiers. They are trained in de-escalation as much as in intervention, and they are known where they serve. Here the asymmetric transparency that runs through the whole work applies. Full transparency applies to power, to every operation, every order, and every means, while the life of the individual is protected by privacy. SOLIS thus reverses the direction of surveillance: it is not the citizen who is exposed and the apparatus that is hidden, but the apparatus that is exposed and the citizen who is protected.

The Exception that Ends Itself

Even an order without a top must be able to act when an emergency leaves no time for deliberation. SOLIS therefore regulates the exception in advance, in a public model of stages that everyone knows. In the normal state, the ordinary rules apply. Heightened alert permits exercises, the replenishing of stocks, and stronger protection of critical facilities. Partial mobilization opens shelters, calls up the reserve, and concentrates forces where the danger lies. Full mobilization, finally, permits evacuations, the redirection of resources, and the opening of aid corridors across borders. Each stage comes with clearly defined powers, is declared and justified in public, and carries an expiry date. Without a new decision of the assembly, it ends by itself.

What distinguishes the exception from emergency rule is the duty to dismantle. After every escalation, whatever it required is withdrawn: powers, collections of data, positions, stocks. A body filled partly by lot reviews every measure afterwards to see whether it was necessary and proportionate. Thus SOLIS can act within hours without the hours turning into years. The emergency receives what it needs and keeps none of it.

External Defence without Attack

Armed power has always been double-edged in history. It has offered protection and brought destruction, secured stability and stoked fear, defended peoples and unleashed wars. In conventional systems, the military often serves national interests, the projection of power, the securing of resources, and influence, and thereby leads to wars of aggression, arms races, and oppression at home. Towards the outside world, SOLIS therefore holds to a distinction that matters: attack is forbidden, defence is legitimate. SOLIS does not establish itself by force and subjugates no one. And yet it is not obliged to let itself be destroyed in the name of tolerance. A society that has built something just may defend it without itself becoming the aggressor.

The doctrine is exclusively defensive. Defence protects the population from attacks, secures critical infrastructure, data, and supplies, and preserves ecosystems and resources. An attack on a power grid is repelled by cyber defence without SOLIS itself escalating. The system is organized in domains, cyberspace and the information space, air and space, land and sea, and civil protection, whose forces work together in a coordinated way. It is carried by a reserve that is trained in modules and pursues its ordinary occupations in everyday life. There are no attack missions, no machine that wages war on its own, and no weapon whose final decision over life does not lie in human hands. An artificial intelligence may detect and classify threats, but it may not make a lethal decision without human approval. The military leadership is always under civilian control and thus, like every power in SOLIS, under the oversight of the Ring and under the law.

Towards the outside world, SOLIS seeks a balance of caution: shared early warning systems, arms control, emergency protocols between the orders, and alliances with those who share its principles, so that a society that binds itself does not stand alone. A capacity for defence exists, sufficient to deter and bound so as not to become a threat itself. Thus SOLIS frees defence from the logic of attack and power projection: no arms race, no war of aggression, no deployment against its own population, but the protection of people and planet.

Earth Defence and Civil Protection

Not every danger wears a uniform. Earthquakes, floods, epidemics, technical accidents, solar storms that threaten the grid, and celestial bodies on a collision course hit people harder than most enemies, and they do not ask about borders. SOLIS therefore joins its protective structures with civilian tasks into an Earth Defence. Dedicated units, a Disaster Corps, are trained for high mobility and respond to natural disasters, epidemics, technical accidents, and threats from space. After an earthquake, they support evacuations, provide emergency power, and secure medical care. An asteroid detected early is deflected while civil protection prepares the evacuation.

Civil protection is built in a decentralized way, like everything in this order that must hold in an emergency: distributed stocks, shelters, emergency energy, connections secured several times over, and people who are trained instead of being patronized. Where the danger strikes all of humanity, the Earth Defence becomes part of the planetary defence that the chapter on the world order deals with. It is carried jointly and is itself ringed in turn, so that protection against the end never becomes a pretext for the rise of any single power.

Peace as Active Work

The deepest protection, however, is not an apparatus but the absence of reasons for violence. In SOLIS, peace is not a pause between conflicts but continuous work: defusing tensions early, remedying injustices before they turn into violence, cultivating understanding across borders, and a diplomacy that does not begin only in a crisis. What a society invests in prevention, education, and justice it does not have to invest in weapons. Thus SOLIS defends itself most effectively not by striking harder, but by removing the fuel from which violence arises. In this order, strength means restraint, not aggression.
