Chapter 04

Security, Defence, and Peace

In the end, every order rests on a question that many designs avoid: who controls force? Since Max Weber, the state has been defined as the entity that claims a monopoly on the legitimate use of force — and whoever controls that monopoly, over police, defence, and ultimate coercion, ultimately controls everything else, however carefully the other bodies are arranged. A Ring that distributes power but leaves force undivided would be a mere façade. Hence the principle of this chapter: security is not the exception to the order, but its sharpest application.

Who Holds the Sword

The temptation is to create, in the name of security, an authority that stands above the rules — in an emergency, for everyone’s protection, for a limited time. This is where almost every freedom in history has died, because exceptional powers, once granted, are rarely returned: the power meant to see a society through the storm outlasts the storm. SOLIS concludes from this that there may be no place where force escapes control. Here too, the answer to the old question of who guards the guardians is not a higher guardian, but the same as everywhere else: a structure in which no one can entrench themselves.

Force Bound into the Ring

Four components bind force, just as four components bind political power. First, civilian oversight by bodies partly chosen by lot: the armed services are watched not by their own kind, but by the citizens. Second, rotation of command and term limits, so that no lasting cartel and no personal following can form around force. Third, the separation of orders from their execution, and of command from review: whoever orders the use of force may not also judge whether it was lawful. Fourth, full traceability of every operation. Above these components stands an immovable limit: the order’s force may never be turned inward against political dissent. Just as the intervention procedure of the media order may never be used against criticism of SOLIS, the security apparatus may never be used against those who oppose the order. This limit prevents the protection of the order from becoming the instrument of its ossification.

Internal Security without a Police State

In SOLIS, security at home does not come first from surveillance and deterrence, but from tackling the causes of crime: where no one is driven into crime by need, exclusion, or hopelessness, crime falls before any patrol goes looking for it. Where protection is still needed, the principle of the mildest means applies: as little coercion as possible, and force only in response to force. The security services are part of the community, not an occupying force. Here the asymmetric transparency that runs through the whole work comes into play: full visibility falls on power — on every operation, every order, every means used — while the life of the individual remains protected by privacy. SOLIS thus reverses the direction of surveillance: it is not the citizen who is exposed and the apparatus that is hidden, but the apparatus that is exposed and the citizen who is protected.

External Defence without Attack

Towards the outside world, one distinction matters: attack is forbidden, defence is legitimate. SOLIS does not impose itself by force and subjugates no one, yet it is not obliged to let itself be destroyed in the name of tolerance. A society that has built something just may defend it without becoming the aggressor. This calls for a defence that is strong enough to deter and bound tightly enough not to become a threat itself: no machine that can wage war on its own, no weapon whose final decision over life and death does not rest in human hands, no armament outside oversight. The capacity to defend exists, but under the same Ring and the same limit as every other form of force.

Peace as Active Work

The deepest protection, however, is not an apparatus but the absence of reasons for violence. In SOLIS, peace is not a pause between conflicts but continuous work: defusing tensions early, remedying injustices before they turn into violence, and cultivating understanding across borders. What a society invests in prevention, education, and justice, it does not have to invest in weapons. SOLIS thus defends itself most effectively not by striking harder, but by removing the tinder from which violence ignites.
