Chapter 11

Children and Families

Birth and Early Childhood

When a child comes into the world, it is not only a biological event but an act of hope, a moment in which a society renews itself, and an invitation to everyone to take responsibility. SOLIS therefore sees birth not as a purely private matter but as a shared task: every child is valuable, not only to its family but to the whole of society.

From the outset, every expectant mother receives high-quality medical, psychological, and social support — prenatal care, help during birth, and aftercare — regardless of income, status, or place of residence, carried by a culture of care in which women are safe and respected. Because children are not raised by mothers alone, all caregivers are included: fathers, co-parents, grandparents, siblings, and chosen families receive advice and support, and the diversity of family forms is recognized. A father does not just get parental leave on paper, but a whole network of support so that he can be truly present at the start of family life.

Early childcare here is a place of loving care and not mere supervision. Small groups, trained professionals, and spaces for play, creativity, and bonding replace rigid timetables, and there is no pressure for early schooling, only activities geared to the child. Because parenthood is often exhausting and lonely, no one is left to cope alone: family centres and midwifery services are fixed points of support, neighbourhood initiatives organize mutual help, and financial and bureaucratic hurdles are deliberately removed, so that a young mother on a low income can also accept support without shame. There are deep reasons for this: the first years of life shape the brain, the body, and the emotions; people who feel supported make better parents; and every child who is raised well is a gain for everyone. A society that surrounds even a person’s first breath with dignity and community shows that its members stand up for one another.

Education and Care

Children are born learners; they ask why before they can talk, and they discover, explore, and play not because they are made to, but because it is their nature. SOLIS shapes education so that this innate joy in learning is not lost but preserved and strengthened. Where today’s school systems try to take all children through the same content at the same pace to the same exams — overwhelming some and under-challenging others — children here learn individually: at their own pace, following their own interests, and building on their own strengths. A child who is fascinated by nature can research and experiment; a child who loves numbers gets extra challenges without being labelled; a child who blossoms in art is supported as a full member of the community and not treated as second-rate.

In this kind of education, mistakes are not a stain but a sign of where there is still something to discover; instead of fear of bad marks and a division into clever and stupid, children are encouraged to try another way, to reflect, and to grow. Teachers are not just transmitters of subject matter but companions and mentors who open up paths of learning and support emotional, social, and creative development — which means less teaching from the front and more shared projects and relationship work. Creativity, cooperation, and social intelligence stand as equals alongside the traditional subjects: art is not a minor subject, teamwork is a key skill, and practising empathy counts as much as factual knowledge.

A single project shows how closely all this fits together. When children build a tree house together, they combine the mathematics of statics and measurement, the biology of suitable trees and nature conservation, the language of documentation, the design sense of art, and the ethics of cooperation and responsibility into living, practical learning that has nothing to do with memorizing isolated facts for a test. An education like this sees the child as a whole person and produces the creative, reflective, and courageous people who can shape change. Above all, however, it ties in with the maturity grounded in the chapter on education: from the very beginning it nurtures independent thinking and the ability to question, instead of drilling compliance — because the challenges of this century cannot be solved with the schools of the century before last.

Family Structures

The family is one of humanity’s oldest and most powerful social structures, yet it is not a rigid model; it changes with time, culture, and living conditions. SOLIS therefore understands the family not as a fixed template but as a living, flexible, and loving network, and it strengthens every kind of family bond that is carried by care, responsibility, and devotion, whatever it looks like.

This removes the invisible norm that, in many of today’s systems, treats only one family form as normal and merely tolerates all others. Same-sex parents, blended families, single parents, chosen families without biological ties, and multigenerational households are recognized as equally valuable, as long as they are based on care and mutual respect; there is no discrimination in rights and benefits, no moral judgment from outside, and no ranking of traditional over modern. At the centre stands not society’s idea of morality but the well-being of the child: support, education, health care, and childcare are geared to what children and their caregivers actually need, not to whether they fit a particular model. A single father has the same rights as a couple, a same-sex couple exercises parenthood jointly without bureaucratic hurdles, and a chosen family of friends raising a child counts as a family in the full sense.

So that families really have time for one another, working life is organized differently. Flexible part-time work, job sharing, a sensible amount of working from home, parental leave for both parents, and support for family carers are not a luxury but a cornerstone of a healthy society; a father can take several months of parental leave without putting his career at risk, and grandparents who spend a lot of time with their grandchildren are recognized as part of the family network. The family is thus relieved rather than overburdened; it is not a private building site that collapses under growing pressure, but something valued and supported by society as a whole. Care and child-rearing are culturally upgraded and recognized as a valuable achievement, not a private sacrifice — and a society that invests in its families builds cohesion, resilience, and emotional health in the long run.

Safe Spaces and Emotional Development

Children need more than education, food, and a roof over their heads; they need emotional security — spaces in which they can make mistakes, learn to trust, express their feelings, and develop resilience. In SOLIS, emotional health is therefore not a side issue but a central goal. This includes stable, reliable adults whom children can turn to with their worries, without first having to make an appointment or go through official channels. Trusted teachers who are visible and approachable, key carers in day nurseries, and neighbourhood centres with open doors give children the certainty that someone is listening and takes them seriously, which reduces fear and helplessness and builds courage and self-confidence. On top of this come programmes for resilience, mindfulness, and empathy that are a firm part of everyday life, because emotional strength is not innate but grows when it is nurtured. Children learn early on to recognize and manage their feelings, to put themselves in other people’s shoes, and to disagree fairly, and they learn that setbacks are part of life and how to deal with them in a healthy way.

Because the digital world is both a playground and a risk for children, media literacy is taught early. A child learns not only how to use a device but also how social media work, how to tell real information from manipulation, and that likes are no measure of their own worth — a direct continuation of the maturity grounded elsewhere in this work. If a child nevertheless becomes the target of bullying, it is not left alone: support structures step in early, and instead of mere punishment there is real relationship work that strengthens the child affected, while recognizing that the child doing the bullying often needs support too — the same restorative spirit that shapes the legal system. SOLIS thus takes the heart as seriously as the mind, because emotionally strong children become reflective and empathetic adults who sustain a healthy society.

Youth, Identity, and Self-Discovery

Youth is a phase like no other. Body and mind change rapidly, and questions come to the fore that were barely there before — who you are, where you belong, what you want to do with your life, and what freedom, responsibility, and meaning really mean. In many societies, this time is seen as a problem phase, and young people are treated as a nuisance or a risk to be controlled. SOLIS, by contrast, sees youth not as a threat but as a valuable time of transition — of searching, trying things out, and growing — that deserves particular attention.

Young people need spaces in which they can discover themselves without being judged or punished straight away, and SOLIS creates them: creative workshops, sports facilities, youth centres, digital platforms, and political and social participation projects. A youth centre therefore offers not just a pool table but workshops for music, environmental projects, start-up ideas, philosophy, and theatre, where young people find out, with peers and adults, what really moves them. They are supported in finding themselves without pressure and without being put down: young people have the right to question, to doubt, and to reinvent themselves, and mentoring, counselling, and peer networks aim to strengthen identity rather than break it — in a climate of trust, respect, and freedom.

This is also the time when sensitive questions come up, for example about sexuality, gender identity, belief, and political views. In SOLIS, these questions may be asked and discussed openly, and young people are given the chance to find their own voice in a safe and respectful setting with competent support. The point is not to steer them in a predetermined direction, but to help them find their own — entirely in the spirit of the maturity and of the multidimensional, fluid understanding of identity developed earlier in this work. This matters, because self-confident young people with good support have better chances of a fulfilled life, and a society that strengthens its young people gains creativity, innovation, and stability. Children and young people are not only future adults; they are already full members of the community with their own rights, needs, and dreams — and an order that recognizes this moves forward together with them.
