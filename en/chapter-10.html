Chapter 10

Technology, Artificial Intelligence, and the Future

In the assembly, the system presented three ways in which the region could harden its grid against the next solar storm, with costs, risks, and how much land each option would eat up. A second system had been instructed to argue against the obvious option, and did it well. The cook asked whether the question had been put correctly: whether this was about the grid or about the people who would be sitting in the dark. The machines had nothing to say to that. That was the moment the assembly began to work.

Human beings have always built tools to help them, from the hand axe to the quantum computer. But every tool asks a question: who is serving whom here? Does technology free people, or does it make them dependent? Does it create prosperity, or does it deepen inequality? Does it make work easier, or does it take away its meaning? Never before have these questions been so urgent, because never before have humanity’s actions determined its own future as deeply, as quickly, and as irreversibly as today. SOLIS faces them and draws one conclusion. Technology is not fate. It is not an unstoppable current that sweeps us away, but a tool whose use we decide on.

Futures Research and Scenario Planning

Climate change, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, the opening up of space, and the shift of power between the world’s regions are shaping a future that can no longer be left to chance. A good society therefore does not only look into the future. It shapes it consciously, ethically, and prepared. To this end, SOLIS maintains its own institutions that think ahead continuously: institutes and research networks that analyse technical, social, and ecological developments and draw up scenarios from them. What happens when artificial intelligence reaches a new level? What would be the consequences of far-reaching automation? In this work, science, enterprises, and the citizenry come together, so that the best minds think ahead jointly.

What is decisive is that this futures research does not happen in secret. There are no shadowy circles that decide everything in advance. Scenarios and analyses are made publicly accessible, taken up by schools and the media, and fed into citizen participation, so that society as a whole thinks along and sets priorities. A town holds a futures forum every year, at which citizens learn about coming developments, argue with experts, and contribute their own ideas. New technologies are therefore assessed technically, ethically, socially, and ecologically, and the public debate is held before a development gets out of control, not only afterwards. Thus the great upheavals are not suffered but shaped together: out of responsible foresight instead of fear of the future.

Technology as a Tool

In SOLIS, powerful technologies, artificial intelligence, robotics, and biotechnology, are ethically examined and actively steered. Every new technology is questioned about its economic benefit and equally about its social, ecological, and psychological consequences. Procedures such as altering the genome, quantum computing, or neural interfaces are not left to market forces alone. Ethical guidelines and public debate accompany them. Dedicated institutions, technology councils as expert bodies, the ethics councils, and citizens’ forums, share in deciding what is developed, what is introduced, and what must be halted or changed. Whatever serves the common good is supported by the funds: an application that makes learning easier for disadvantaged children, a storage system that carries the energy transition. Whatever only exploits attention without contributing anything is limited.

Above everything stands a clear basic attitude. Technology should make people stronger, freer, and more creative, not weaker, more dependent, and more uniform. The human being remains sovereign. Machines do not steer people. People steer machines. Algorithms do not write the laws. People write algorithms that respect the laws. Biotechnology does not determine the future of the species. People decide which interventions are ethically justifiable. What we decide in the coming decades helps determine whether humanity slides into a world of surveillance and dehumanization or rises into a world of possibilities and new forms of meaning. SOLIS takes the helm. It uses technology without letting itself be used by it.

Artificial Intelligence and Humanity

Artificial intelligence is no ordinary tool. It is able to learn, improves itself, may become autonomous, and may be more powerful than any human institution. In conventional systems it is seen either as a pure means of efficiency or feared as a threat. SOLIS takes a third path. Artificial intelligence can be a participant in the development of humanity, but it may never rule, replace, or incapacitate human beings. It is meant to support and strengthen them.

Clear ethical limits apply to this. Artificial intelligence may take on tasks that are too complex, too dangerous, or too monotonous for people, but never the final responsibility over life, dignity, and freedom. It may not wage war on its own, may not manipulate anyone unnoticed, and may not replace any human relationship. An artificial intelligence in a hospital can allocate beds and resources, but it does not decide on its own about a life-saving treatment. That decision remains human. Especially powerful systems are subject to strict, collective, and international oversight and are never controlled by individuals, enterprises, or single orders alone, a direct application of the order of power that permits no top and no monopoly over a central resource. Provision has been made for the extreme case. Every powerful system has a switch that people hold, and the ability to stop it is practised like the emergency itself.

Wherever artificial intelligence acts in public areas, in administration, education, health, or the courts, it must be able to explain its decisions. There may be no black box whose logic no one understands. Those affected have a right to inspection, to explanation, and to objection, and independent bodies review the systems regularly. Thus the separation of knowledge and decision is preserved, and the human being keeps the last word. Above all this stands a broad public conversation about how far artificial intelligence may go and what makes us human, held throughout society and not only in expert bodies, so that the most powerful tool in history does not become the master of its creators.

The Human above the Machine

That the human being keeps the last word is easy to state as a principle and hard to secure. An artificial intelligence far superior to humans can dominate them without ever giving an order: by pre-sorting the situation, selecting the options, and preparing the decision so that in the end all that remains is a nod. SOLIS meets this silent capture with firm safeguards. For every far-reaching decision, the system does not present one recommendation as if there were no alternative, but several genuinely different paths together with their trade-offs. A justification that even a layperson can check takes the place of blind trust, and review systems appointed specifically for the purpose have the task of arguing against the obvious recommendation. Above all, however, the framing of the question remains reserved to humans, the determination of what is at stake and what counts as success. The machine illuminates the answer. The human asks the question.

The Digital Commons

The digital sphere is the nervous system of modern society. Almost everything runs through networks, data, and algorithms today: communication, the economy, knowledge, and power. In many of today’s systems, this nervous system is privately controlled. A few corporations control the platforms, data are exploited like raw materials, and algorithms decide invisibly what we see and think. SOLIS, by contrast, understands the digital order as public infrastructure and not as private property. It serves people, not the other way round.

The basic digital infrastructure is therefore a common good. Networks, data spaces, and basic technologies are open to all, managed and secured collectively, dominated by no monopoly, and access is a right and not a privilege. A person in a remote region receives the same basic digital provision as someone in a big city. Linked to this is control over one’s own data. Every person decides for themselves who may use their data, there is no secret exploitation and no trade in what is private, and whoever uses an application keeps control and can see and revoke at any time what is happening. To this is added digital transparency. Public data platforms make projects and decisions traceable, algorithms that affect the community are disclosed and open to review, and control lies with society and not in the dark, so that every citizen can understand how a public allocation procedure decides. Thus the digital nervous system is freed from private control: no exploitation of data, no invisible steering, no technology that rules over people. The digital belongs to everyone, data belong to the individual, and technology serves people and not power.

Asymmetric Transparency

The openness on which this order relies carries a danger within it. The same infrastructure of data and sensors that makes power transparent could expose the citizen. Pointed in the wrong direction, transparency would become a panopticon. SOLIS therefore deliberately reverses its direction. Full transparency applies to power, to the offices, the funds, and the algorithms, while the life of the individual is protected by privacy. Privacy is a right, data minimization a rule, and each person remains the master of the data about them. Whoever exercises power over others becomes visible. Whoever only lives their life remains hidden.

Transhumanism and Consciousness

The history of humanity is a history of extension, from the tool that lengthens the hand to the computer that accelerates thinking. Now, however, we stand at a new point, at which technologies no longer change only what we use but what we are: neural interfaces, implants, interventions in the genome, assistance systems closely connected with us. SOLIS faces this future neither naively nor fanatically. It faces it in the awareness that it is opportunity and risk at the same time and demands the utmost care.

Technologies that extend body and mind are therefore examined strictly on ethical grounds, without a blanket ban but without naive enthusiasm. Every new possibility is examined in many dimensions, technically and medically as well as ethically, socially, and philosophically, and may be introduced only if it guarantees safety, respects dignity and identity, and preserves fairness. An implant that gives a blind person back their sight is supported. An intervention that would give a wealthy minority mental superiority is slowed down, because it entrenches inequality. It follows directly that there may be no compulsion to be enhanced and no two-class society. Participation, dignity, and rights never depend on technical improvements. Whoever does not want to be enhanced remains a full member of the community. No job and no opportunity may be tied to an implant, and no child may be disadvantaged because their parents decided against an intervention. Here the absolute floor of fundamental rights takes effect. Dignity comes before any optimization.

Because these questions touch on how human beings understand themselves, their relationships, and their mortality, philosophical and ethical reflection accompanies every shift of the limit. Research into whether consciousness can be digitized or extended is not dogmatically forbidden either, but it is conducted with the utmost respect for identity and meaning and bound to clear limits that prevent misuse and alienation. The question of whether we should do something is always taken as seriously as the question of whether we can. Thus humanity can grow at this threshold without losing itself.

Space Research and Interplanetary Ethics

For thousands of years, human beings have looked up to the stars with longing, and today this gaze is no longer a dream. Moon bases are being planned, missions to Mars prepared, the mining of raw materials on asteroids developed. This raises the question of how humanity deals with this new stage. SOLIS gives a clear answer. Space does not belong to individual nations or corporations but is a shared heritage and an extended living space of all humanity. Research in space is therefore conducted internationally, cooperatively, and peacefully. Large projects are joint missions of many orders and disciplines, not races, scientific exchange and open invention come first, and the military use of space is ruled out. The mining of raw materials is permitted only under clear ecological, ethical, and fair rules. The consequences are examined and limited, the yields benefit the whole of humanity through joint funds and not only those who get there first, and the planetary level secures approval and oversight. A consortium that wants to open up an asteroid must therefore show that there is no ecological risk and that part of the yield flows back into common goods.

Space, moreover, is more than a store of raw materials. It is a possible living space and perhaps the home of other forms of life. SOLIS therefore considers from the outset how to deal with possible extraterrestrial life, how to prevent organisms from being carried in either direction, and how fair participation is kept secure. Ethicists and philosophers therefore work in the research teams too, there are clear protocols for dealing with the unknown, and humanity appears as a united civilization and not as a quarrelling crowd of competing groups. Thus it goes into space not as a reckless conqueror but as a learning and responsible species.

Risk and Disaster Management

A society that builds only on good times builds on sand. Natural disasters, global crises, technical dangers, and threats from space are part of the reality of a connected and technically complex civilization. SOLIS does not accept this fatalistically. It acts. Risks can never be avoided entirely, but one can prepare for them and build systems that are robust, able to learn, and adaptable.

This includes structures that monitor and assess risks continuously: interdisciplinary teams from science, technology, and medicine, early warning systems that evaluate large amounts of data in real time, and systematic scenario planning. It is not an exercise in fear. It is the basis of targeted preparation. If such a system detects an unusual threat early, assessments are drawn up within hours, the stages of Earth Defence are declared, and resources are redirected. Disaster protection is a shared project and not national egoism. Early warning systems are connected internationally, emergency plans exist at every level, and aid networks are prepared, rehearsed, and equipped before the emergency occurs. If a solar storm threatens the power grid, the distributed energy supply absorbs the failure, and connections secured several times over hold the community together.

Resilience, however, is more than technology. It is social, psychological, and ecological at the same time: strong communities and neighbourhoods, informed and composed people, and landscapes and towns that can cushion crises. After a disaster, people can therefore draw on material help and equally on community structures that carry them emotionally. Thus people feel not like helpless victims but like co-responsible members of a resilient society that emerges from strain stronger than before, true to the resilience that belongs to the foundations of this order.

Humanity’s Place in the Cosmos

At the end of all technology and all systems stands not only the question of what we can do but the greater question of who we are and what we want to be: for ourselves, for one another, and for the universe. SOLIS answers it not with arrogance but with humility. We are not the masters of the universe and not the crown of creation. We are part of an immeasurably large and still largely unknown whole. And it is precisely from this insight that a great responsibility grows. The old logic held that what we can take belongs to us and that whoever is stronger decides. SOLIS reverses this logic. Where we use resources beyond the Earth, we do so as careful stewards and not as blind exploiters. Where we encounter other life, we come not as conquerors but as attentive fellow players. Where we explore the cosmos, we do so aware of the fragility of all that lives. Our significance is measured not by power but by responsibility, and not by what we take but by what we contribute.

Technical progress and expansion are meaningful only if they go hand in hand with inner growth, with wisdom, compassion, and mindfulness. That is why SOLIS measures greatness not by expansion but by maturity. We do not settle worlds just because we can. We first ask what for. We develop technology that increases not only our reach but also our capacity for reflection. We build a culture distinguished not by its size but by its depth. A ship travelling for generations to a distant star would be not only a technical adventure but the question of how to carry community, meaning, and humanity across centuries. The great aim is not faster, higher, mightier, richer. It is to become a civilization that gives the cosmos more than it takes from it, that carries culture, beauty, and knowledge outwards, that learns instead of only conquering, and that leaves traces that shine not only technically but morally. Perhaps we are the first known species that can decide consciously how it behaves in the universe. We should not squander this chance.
