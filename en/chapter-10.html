Chapter 10

Technology, Artificial Intelligence, and the Future

Futures Research and Scenario Planning

Never before have humanity’s own actions shaped its future so deeply, so quickly, and so irreversibly as today. A changing climate, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, the opening up of space, and shifts in the geopolitical balance are shaping a future that can no longer be left to chance. SOLIS concludes from this that a good society does not merely look into the future, but shapes it deliberately, ethically, and with preparation.

To this end, SOLIS maintains dedicated institutions that continually think ahead — institutes and research alliances that analyse technological, social, and ecological developments and draw up concrete scenarios from them: What happens when artificial intelligence reaches a new stage? What would far-reaching automation mean? In this work, science, business, and civil society come together, so that the best minds think ahead jointly. What matters is that this futures research does not happen in secret. There are no shadow circles that decide everything in advance; scenarios and analyses are made publicly available, taken up by schools and the media, and fed into citizen participation, so that society as a whole thinks along and sets priorities.

From this follows the principle that technology is not fate. It is not an unstoppable current that sweeps us along, but a tool whose use we decide. New technologies are therefore assessed not only technically, but also ethically, socially, and ecologically, and the public debate takes place before a development gets out of control, not only afterwards. The great upheavals are thus not simply endured but actively shaped — not out of fear of the future, but out of responsible foresight.

Artificial Intelligence and Humanity

Artificial intelligence is not an ordinary tool. It can learn and optimize itself, it may become independent, and it could be more powerful than any human institution. Traditional systems either see it purely as a means of efficiency or fear it as a threat. SOLIS takes a third path: artificial intelligence can be a partner in humanity’s development, yet it may never dominate, replace, or disempower human beings; it should support and strengthen them.

Clear ethical limits apply. Artificial intelligence may take over tasks that are too complex, too dangerous, or too monotonous for people, but never final responsibility for life, dignity, and freedom; it may not wage war on its own, manipulate anyone without their knowledge, or replace any human relationship. An artificial intelligence in a hospital can therefore allocate beds and resources optimally, but it does not decide on its own about a life-saving treatment; that decision remains human. Particularly powerful systems are subject to strict, shared, and international oversight and are never controlled by individuals, corporations, or single states alone — a direct application of the order of power, which allows no summit and no monopoly over a central resource.

Wherever artificial intelligence acts in public domains — administration, education, health, or justice — it must be able to explain its decisions. There may be no black box whose logic no one understands; those affected have a right to see, to receive an explanation, and to appeal, and independent bodies review the systems regularly. In this way, the separation of knowledge from decision is preserved, and human beings keep the last word. Above all this stands a broad public debate about how far artificial intelligence may go and what makes us human — held not only in expert bodies but across society as a whole, so that the most powerful tool in history does not become the master of its creators.

Transhumanism and Consciousness

The history of humanity is a history of extension — from the tool that lengthens the hand to the computer that speeds up thought. But now we have reached a new point, at which technologies no longer change only what we use, but what we are: neural interfaces, implants, genetic interventions, assistive systems closely bound up with our bodies. SOLIS faces this future neither naively nor fanatically, but in the knowledge that it is both an opportunity and a risk and demands the utmost care.

Technologies that extend body and mind are therefore subject to strict ethical scrutiny — without a blanket ban, but also without naive enthusiasm. Every new possibility is examined in many dimensions, technical and medical as well as ethical, social, and philosophical, and may be introduced only if it guarantees safety, respects dignity and identity, and preserves fairness in society. An implant that restores sight to a blind person is supported; an intervention that would give a wealthy minority cognitive superiority is restrained, because it entrenches inequality. It follows directly that there may be no pressure to enhance and no two-tier society: participation, dignity, and rights never depend on technical enhancements; anyone who does not want to be enhanced remains a full member of the community; and no job and no opportunity may be tied to an implant. This is where the absolute floor of fundamental rights takes effect: dignity comes before any optimization.

Because these questions touch on how people understand themselves, their relationships, and their mortality, every shift of the boundary is accompanied by philosophical and ethical reflection. Research into whether consciousness can be digitized or extended is not dogmatically forbidden either, but is pursued with the utmost respect for identity and meaning and bound by clear limits that prevent misuse and alienation. The question of whether we should do something is always taken as seriously here as the question of whether we can. In this way, humanity can grow at this threshold without losing itself.

Space Research and Interplanetary Ethics

For thousands of years, human beings have looked up at the stars with longing, and today that gaze is no longer a dream: lunar bases are being planned, missions to Mars prepared, and the mining of asteroids developed. This raises the question of how humanity will handle this new stage. SOLIS gives a clear answer: space does not belong to individual nations or corporations, but is a shared heritage and an extended living space for all of humanity. Research in space is therefore international, cooperative, and peaceful. Major undertakings are joint missions involving many countries and disciplines, not geopolitical races; scientific exchange and open innovation come first; and any military use of space is ruled out without exception. Mining is permitted only under clear ecological, ethical, and fair rules: environmental consequences are examined and minimized, the proceeds benefit all of humanity through shared funds rather than private investors alone, and international institutions handle approval and oversight. A consortium that wants to mine an asteroid must therefore show that there is no ecological risk and that part of the profits flows back into common goods.

Space is not only a store of raw materials but a possible living space and perhaps home to other forms of life. From the outset, SOLIS therefore considers how to deal with possible extraterrestrial life, how to prevent organisms from being carried across in either direction, and how to keep participation fair. Ethicists and philosophers also work in the research teams, there are clear protocols for dealing with the unknown, and humanity appears as a united civilization rather than a quarrelling mass of competing groups. It goes into space not as a ruthless conqueror, but as a learning and responsible species.

Risk and Disaster Management

A society that builds only on good times builds on sand. Natural disasters, global crises, technological dangers, and threats from space are part of the reality of an interconnected and technically complex civilization. SOLIS does not accept this fatalistically but responds actively: risks can never be avoided entirely, yet we can prepare for them and build systems that are robust, able to learn, and adaptable.

This includes structures that continually monitor and assess risks — cross-disciplinary teams from science, technology, and medicine; early-warning systems that analyse large amounts of data in real time; and systematic scenario planning that is not an exercise in fear but the basis for targeted preparation. When such a system spots an unusual threat early, assessments are drawn up within hours, emergency plans are activated, and resources are redirected. Disaster protection here is not national self-interest but a shared project: early-warning systems are linked internationally, emergency plans exist at every level, and aid networks are prepared, trained, and funded before an emergency occurs. If a solar storm threatens the power grid, for example, a decentralized energy supply absorbs the outage, and communication channels with multiple backups keep people connected.

Resilience, however, is more than technology. It is social, psychological, and ecological at once: strong communities and neighbourhoods, informed and level-headed people, and landscapes and cities that can cushion crises. After a disaster, people can therefore rely not only on material help but on community structures that provide psychological support. People thus experience themselves not as helpless victims but as responsible members of a resilient society that emerges from strain all the stronger — true to the resilience that is one of the foundations of this order.

Humanity’s Place in the Cosmos

Beyond all technology and all systems lies not only the question of what we can do, but the greater question of who we are and who we want to be — for ourselves, for one another, and for the universe. SOLIS answers it not with arrogance but with humility. We are not the lords of the universe and not the crown of creation, but part of an immeasurably vast and still largely unknown whole. And it is precisely from this insight that a great responsibility grows. In the old logic, whatever we could take belonged to us, and the stronger decided. SOLIS reverses this logic. Where we use resources beyond Earth, we do so as careful stewards and not as blind exploiters; where we encounter alien life, we come not as conquerors but as mindful partners; and where we explore the cosmos, we do so aware of how fragile all life is. Our significance is measured not by power but by responsibility, not by what we take but by what we contribute. Technological progress and expansion make sense only when they go hand in hand with inner growth — with wisdom, empathy, and mindfulness. SOLIS therefore measures greatness not by expansion but by maturity. We do not settle worlds merely because we can, but first ask what for; we develop technology that extends not only our reach but also our capacity for reflection; and we build a culture that stands out not for its size but for its depth. The great aim is not faster, higher, mightier, richer. It is to become a civilization that gives the cosmos more than it takes from it — one that carries culture, beauty, and knowledge outward, that learns instead of merely conquering, and that leaves traces that shine not only technically but morally. Perhaps we are the first known species that can consciously decide how it will conduct itself in the universe. That is a chance we should not waste.

Technology and Digitalization

The digital world is the nervous system of modern society; almost everything today runs through networks, data, and algorithms — communication, the economy, knowledge, and power. In many of today’s systems, this nervous system is dominated by private interests: a few corporations control the platforms, data are exploited like raw materials, and algorithms invisibly decide what we see and think. SOLIS, by contrast, understands the digital order as public infrastructure, not private property: it serves people, not the other way round.

The basic digital infrastructure is therefore a common good. Networks, data spaces, and core technologies are open to everyone, are not dominated by monopolies but publicly managed and secured, and access is a right, not a privilege — so that someone in a remote region receives the same basic digital provision as someone in a big city. This goes hand in hand with control over one’s own data: every person decides for themselves who may use their data; there is no secret exploitation and no trade in private information; and anyone who uses an application stays in control and can see and revoke at any time what happens with their data. Data serve people, not their manipulation. Where artificial intelligence operates in the digital sphere, what was set out in the section on artificial intelligence applies: it remains an adviser and does not become a ruler, final responsibility remains human, and its systems are explainable and bound to the basic values. Added to this is digital transparency: public data platforms make projects and decisions traceable, algorithms that affect the common good are disclosed and open to review, and control lies with society rather than behind closed doors — so that, for instance, any citizen can follow how a public procurement procedure reaches its decision. The digital nervous system is thus freed from private domination: no exploitation of data, no invisible steering, no technology that rules over people. The digital belongs to everyone, data belong to the individual, and technology serves people, not power.

The Human above the Machine

That humans keep the last word is easy to state as a principle and hard to secure in practice. An artificial intelligence far superior to humans can control them without ever giving an order — by pre-sorting the situation, selecting the options, and preparing the decision so that in the end all that is left is a nod. SOLIS counters this quiet capture with firm safeguards. For every far-reaching decision, the system does not present a single recommendation as if there were no alternative, but several genuinely different paths together with their trade-offs; a justification that a layperson can check takes the place of blind trust; and review systems appointed for this purpose have the task of arguing against the obvious recommendation. Above all, framing the question remains reserved for humans — deciding what is up for choice and what counts as success. The machine illuminates the answer; the human asks the question.

Asymmetric Transparency

The openness on which this order rests carries a danger of its own. The same infrastructure of data and sensors that makes power transparent could also expose the citizen; pointed in the wrong direction, transparency would become a panopticon. SOLIS therefore deliberately reverses its direction: full visibility falls on power — on offices, funds, and algorithms — while the life of the individual is protected by privacy. Privacy is a right, data minimization a rule, and every person remains in control of the data that concern them. Whoever exercises power over others becomes visible; whoever simply lives their life remains private.
