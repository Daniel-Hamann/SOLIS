Chapter 03

Law and Justice

The dispute over the noise from the workshop below Mara’s flat did not end in court. It ended at a kitchen table, with a mediator from the neighbourhood, a plan for working hours, and a door that the carpenter soundproofed. Two years later, Mara herself sat on a bench of judges, drawn by lot like the cook beside her. A man had set fire to a warehouse, out of rage, because his enterprise had passed him over. He was not locked away. He helped rebuild the warehouse, for a year, and he came to know the watchman who had almost died that night.

An order reveals its character not in those who follow it but in those who break it. Dealing with wrongdoing is therefore not a fringe area of law but its real test: it shows whether a society’s principles still apply when they become inconvenient. In many existing systems, people experience the justice system as opaque and overly bureaucratic, as dependent on money and influence, and as geared to punishment instead of restoration. Proceedings drag on for years and in the end leave only losers. SOLIS does not answer the question of wrongdoing with the reflex of retribution. It answers with a law that protects, restores, and preserves the dignity even of the person who violated it.

The Dual Structure of Ethics and Law

A society is only as just as its law. Values set the direction. Without institutions that pronounce and enforce the law, they remain theory. SOLIS therefore joins ethics and law into a dual structure that is already laid out in the Ring: the ethics councils examine whether laws and far-reaching decisions are compatible with the five foundations and halt whatever contradicts them. The courts apply the rules bindingly and equally to everyone. One side asks whether a law is good, the other whether it is kept, and neither replaces the other. Laws serve to make the foundations concrete. They may never replace them and never bend them. The result is an order that is not only formally correct but also morally grounded, and that places at its centre not power but balance.

A Law that Everyone Understands

Law in SOLIS is accessible. It may not be a secret code that only experts can decipher. The essential rules, rights, and procedures are available in clear language, are taught in schools, and can be found digitally. Anyone who gets into a conflict can see without a long search which rights they have, which steps are open to them, and where they can find help. Legal assistance is guaranteed, regardless of wealth. Law in SOLIS is also transparent: no judgment is issued without a public justification, and no procedure without verifiable standards. Whoever judges discloses the criteria by which they decide, so that those involved can understand why something was decided, even if they do not like the result. For trust arises only where people know that they are not being judged by caprice or hidden interests.

The Limits of Retribution

The obvious answer to wrongdoing is retribution: whoever causes harm should suffer harm. It is old and understandable, and it falls short. The act that was repaid has still happened, the victim’s loss remains unhealed, and the person punished returns, burdened further by the hardship suffered, to the circumstances from which the act arose. An order that draws its security from punishment alone therefore produces not calm but repetition: overcrowded prisons, high rates of reoffending, and whole groups who shuttle between crime and prison. For SOLIS, a sober principle follows from this. Punishment is not an end but a means, and it is justified only by what it achieves.

Restorative Justice

In place of retribution comes restorative justice. Its measure is not the suffering inflicted but the suffering healed, and it pursues three aims at once. First, redress for the victim: where harm can be made good, making it good takes priority, and the person harmed receives real compensation instead of a symbolic verdict. Second, the return of the offender: where an act arose from need, addiction, or illness, removing its cause is added to the sanction, because whoever leaves the cause untouched invites repetition. Third, the protection of others: deprivation of liberty remains the means for dealing with those who pose a serious danger. But it too serves safety and return, not revenge. Thus punishment is measured by its effect, not by its harshness.

Whoever causes harm is not simply locked away. They are drawn into a process that lets them understand the consequences of their actions and involves them in repairing them. A young person who causes damage to property is not branded but included in a programme that understands them and lets them share in the repair. An enterprise that violates ecological standards does not merely pay a fine but contributes actively to restoration, together with experts and the communities affected. Repressive punishment often fights only symptoms. SOLIS aims at the causes, because that is where the lasting effect lies.

Fair Procedure and Equality before the Law

So that law does not turn into caprice, it rests on procedural guarantees that yield to no expediency: the presumption of innocence, the burden of proof on the prosecution, and the right to defence, to a hearing, and to independent review. Whoever is wrongly accused receives complete protection and assistance. What is decisive, however, is a principle that separates SOLIS from every justice of the rulers: the law applies equally to everyone, and it also applies to the bodies of the Ring. The courts apply the same standards to the Council of the Wise, the ethics councils, the assemblies, and every office holder as to every citizen. No office and no institution stands above the law. A law that spares the powerful is no longer law but a tool.

Open Case Law on the Foundations

The five foundations form the non-negotiable core of the order. Their application in the individual case is not. What dignity requires when two dignities collide, or what responsibility for the planet demands against the freedom of the individual, is decided in interpretation, and interpretation is power. It follows that SOLIS does not leave it to a closed circle of interpreters. Case law on the foundations is open and adversarial. Hard cases are argued by representatives of the opposing sides before a bench filled partly by lot, every decision is justified in public and the dissenting opinion is published with it, and the precedent remains open to challenge. In the most fundamental questions, a citizens’ panel chosen by lot takes part, so that the interpretation of the core stays anchored in the citizenry. Thus the foundations are immovable and their reading is nevertheless open to review. No one has the last word over them.

Disputes between Citizens

Not every wrong is a crime. Most of it is conflict. Where an order forces every dispute straight into court, it turns people into opponents and courts into factories. Law in SOLIS is therefore tiered. At the start come mediation and dialogue, locally, quickly, and regardless of the wealth of those involved. Trained mediators recognize tensions early and accompany the search for agreement. A dispute in the neighbourhood over noise, rubbish, or a misunderstanding between cultures is settled before it escalates into a complaint to the police. Only where agreement fails or rights are touched in principle does a court decide, and for questions that concern the order itself, a final instance. Even children learn to voice conflicts instead of suppressing them or fighting them out with violence. Thus law remains accessible, and disputes more often end in a workable solution than in a winner and a loser.

Who Administers Justice

The system is carried by judges and mediators who do not rise by the logic of careers or power. They are appointed by suitability in an open procedure, undergo a thorough professional, ethical, and psychological examination, train continuously, and are regularly reviewed by the ethics councils and by bodies filled partly by lot. Their independence is protected: no office and no assembly can remove a judge because of a judgment. Yet they too do not sit in one place for life. They move between courts and levels, their presidencies are time-limited, and their work is reviewed in public. Citizens chosen by lot sit on the benches, so that law does not become the business of a caste but remains the judgment of the community. For a legal system is only as strong as the integrity, judgment, and empathy of those who apply it.

Above everything stands the principle that links law to the order of power: no one stands above the law, no office holder and no institution, not the Council of the Wise and not the ethics councils. Law is thus not a tool of the powerful but a shield for the weak. There is no class justice in which wealth buys its way out, no opaque procedures without justification, and no law that only punishes instead of healing the causes.

The Limit of Coercion

Above everything stands a limit that even the guilty keep. No punishment may violate human dignity. There is no torture, no degrading treatment, and no punishment that excludes a person permanently from the community. Every sanction is proportionate, time-limited, open to review, and aimed at return. This limit is not a concession to leniency but a consequence of the foundation: dignity, which in the first foundation knows no condition, does not know the condition of guilt either.
