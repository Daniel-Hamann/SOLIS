Chapter 01

Foundation

The Dead End of the Established Orders

Over the course of its history, humanity has produced several great designs for social order, and each of them achieved something real. Capitalism unleashed prosperity and innovation on a scale without precedent — and at the same time plundered the natural foundations of life and divided societies. Socialism took the promise of equality seriously — and, as actually practised, stifled freedom and inventiveness. Democracy gave people a voice — and reaches its limit where many voices turn into directionless noise that has no means of correcting itself. Autocracy demonstrated resolve and order — at the cost of the dignity and creativity without which no society stays alive.

None of these designs is simply wrong. Each has reached an inner limit that its own logic cannot carry it beyond. These limits stand out so sharply today because the conditions under which those orders arose have changed fundamentally. The great challenges of the present are no longer national, no longer separate from one another, and no longer slow. A changing climate, the concentration of wealth, the upheaval brought by digital technology, and the erosion of a shared relationship to truth interlock, reinforce one another, and unfold faster than established institutions can respond. An order built for a slower, more bounded world can no longer reliably govern a connected and accelerated one. Against this background, SOLIS sees itself not as yet another ideology, but as the answer to a sober analysis of what is missing. That answer comes down to four structural necessities. First, we need an order that brings people, the planet, and technology into balance instead of subordinating one to another — not humanity above nature, not the machine above the human being, not the economy above everything else. Second, we need an order that does not maximize short-term gain but aims at durability, resilience, and shared well-being; growth that consumes the very foundations that make it possible is not progress. Third, we need an order that fosters innovation without abandoning its ethical limits — one that puts the most powerful tools of our time, from artificial intelligence to genetic engineering, in the service of human beings rather than handing human beings over to them. Fourth, we need an order that unites competence and conscience — not through coercion, money, or the accumulation of power, but through meaning and a shared sense of direction; the deepest transformations in history were never merely technical, but always cultural and ethical movements as well.

The obvious objection is that such a design is utopian. Yet it rests not on the assumption that humanity will become better, but on the insight that carrying on as before is what is truly improbable. In a world whose ecological, technological, and geopolitical fate is shared, continuing the existing order is not the safe option but the riskiest of all. SOLIS builds on the resilient elements of human experience — inventiveness, ethical depth, and the capacity to think beyond one’s own immediate interests — and assembles them into an order that can withstand the conditions of this century.

Humanity at the Crossroads

That the established orders are reaching their limits is not an abstract diagnosis. It shows in several crises unfolding at once, each serious in its own right; only in the way they are coupled does the full extent of the danger become visible.

The ecological crisis is the most fundamental, because it concerns the material precondition of every society. An economy that consumes resources faster than they can regenerate, and that treats a stable climate as a free endowment, undermines the conditions of its own survival. No prosperity and no progress can be secured in the long run on a destabilized planet.

The social crisis shows in a concentration of wealth and opportunity that hollows out the cohesion of societies. Where the gap between those who take part and those who are shut out grows too wide, the promise of equality before the law loses its tangible basis, and with it the trust on which every order depends. The technological crisis is of a different kind, because it changes not only our tools but human beings themselves. Today, digital systems shape what people perceive, how they judge, and where they direct their attention. Where these systems serve only to exploit attention, a quiet form of steering emerges that escapes conscious control.

The political crisis follows from the others. Where institutions can no longer cope with the problems, trust wanes; where trust wanes, simple answers gain power — answers that do not resolve complex situations but conceal them. Stable democracies begin to falter, and authoritarian models gain appeal because they promise the ability to act.

Finally, the crisis of meaning is the least visible, and yet deeply consequential. An order that addresses people above all as consumers, workers, and data points leaves unanswered a question that will not be silenced: what is all of it for? Without convincing answers to that question, the inner cohesion from which societies draw their resilience falls apart.

These crises cannot be solved one at a time or one after another, because each of them conditions the others. This is precisely the case for an integrated design: an order that treats ecology, economy, law, education, culture, and technology as separate departments will fail at the interfaces, where the real problems lie. SOLIS therefore does not begin with a single reform, but with the connections between the domains.

The Five Ethical Foundations

Every lasting order needs a base that lies deeper than its laws, its technology, and its institutions, because these change while the base has to bear the weight. In SOLIS this base is ethical, and it consists of five foundations. They are introduced here not as a creed but as working principles: each of them rules out certain kinds of order and entails specific institutional consequences. Human Dignity. At the centre stands the human being as an end in itself, never as a means — regardless of origin, gender, age, belief, or ability. This does not mean that all human beings are the same, but it does mean that they are equal in worth. An order that takes this principle seriously may not divide people into the useful and the useless, and may not abandon anyone for being deemed unproductive. The institutional consequence is a basic security that does not depend on a person’s employment history, and a body of law that protects the weak as well as the strong.

The Balance between Individual and Community. The human being is neither an isolated lone fighter nor a mere component of the collective, but a social individual who needs freedom in order to grow and community in order to flourish. The freedom of the individual ends where it destroys the common good; the common good may not be defined in a way that crushes the dignity of the individual. This balance is not static; it has to be struck again and again — which requires institutions that carry out exactly this weighing instead of settling it one-sidedly.

Responsibility for the Planet. The Earth is not a backdrop and not a mere store of raw materials, but the shared world on which every society depends. This entails an obligation to use it only in ways that allow regeneration, to exercise the greatest caution when intervening in ecosystems, and to protect the natural foundations of life as a core task, not a side issue. This principle is not a moral afterthought but a condition of survival, and it is the reason why, in SOLIS, the natural foundations of life are placed beyond private ownership.

The Advancement of Knowledge and Wisdom. SOLIS bases its decisions not on power, money, or ideology, but on the best available knowledge, joined with ethical judgment. Knowledge without ethics can be destructive; power without knowledge is blind; only the two together can bear weight. This entails an obligation to ongoing research, to testing assumptions, and to open access to knowledge — and equally the recognition that cleverness alone is not enough, but needs the foresight and humility we call wisdom.

Long-term Resilience. The worth of an order is proven not in calm times but in crisis. SOLIS is therefore built for resilience: able to learn, because it can correct itself; robust, because it is decentralized; adaptable, because it takes feedback seriously. In a future marked by ecological, geopolitical, and technological uncertainty, this order should not merely endure but emerge from strain all the stronger.

These five foundations are the standard by which every subsequent chapter can be measured. They are the compass that guides individual decisions, and at the same time the condition that turns many separate rules into a coherent order rather than a patchwork.

Method and Structure of the Work

The ambition of this work calls for a particular method, and it is worth setting that method out before the individual domains are developed.

The first methodological commitment is to pair every principle with an institution. A value that is not translated into a testable rule, a procedure, or an institution remains without consequence. This work therefore does not settle for describing desirable states of affairs; wherever possible, it names the mechanisms that make them last — including, and especially, for the case in which individual actors try to exploit the order. Whenever a principle is introduced, the question of its institutional safeguard must follow.

The second commitment is to apply the order to itself, again and again. The hardest question for any social order is not how it governs its citizens, but how it limits its own power. SOLIS answers this question not with a supreme body of control that would itself remain uncontrolled, but by applying to its own institutions the same principles it lays down for society as a whole — transparency, separation of powers, accountability to the citizens, and protection against capture. This idea underpins the chapter on the order of power in particular, and it recurs throughout the work. On this basis, the structure of the work unfolds. This first chapter lays the ethical foundation. It is followed by the model of society with its order of power, fundamental rights, and law; the order of the economy and resources; education and knowledge; health and well-being; culture and meaning; and the questions of technology, artificial intelligence, and humanity’s place in the wider scheme of things. Then come implementation and the transition strategies that describe the path from what exists today to this order, as well as a dedicated chapter on children and families as the foundation on which every lasting change rests. A final chapter draws the principles together and looks ahead.

The domains build on one another, yet each can also be understood on its own, so the work can be read from start to finish or consulted selectively. It is not a text meant to reassure, but a design meant to be engaged with — written for everyone who believes that more is possible than merely administering what exists: an order that is fundamentally different and at the same time resilient.

The Narrow Core and the Wide Freedom

One misunderstanding needs to be cleared up. That SOLIS rests on five non-negotiable foundations does not mean that it prescribes what a good life consists of. The foundations are a narrow, firm core; above it lies a wide freedom. What meaning, happiness, and fulfilment are, and which paths lead to them, is decided not by the order but by each person and each community for themselves. An order that prescribed this too would no longer be a framework, but a worldview raising itself to the status of law. SOLIS therefore strictly separates what holds for everyone from what belongs to each: a narrow, firm ground of the non-negotiable, and above it the whole breadth of human variety.
