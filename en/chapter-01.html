Chapter 01

Foundation

Imagine you are standing on a high plateau. A wide landscape stretches out before you, and in every direction run paths that were worn down long ago: the broad road of capitalism, the beaten track of socialism, the winding mosaic paths of democracy, the stony marching routes of the autocracies. Each of these paths bears the footprints of whole generations, and each has its own history. And each of them, if you look closely, ends at the same wall.

We sense this wall even when we do not name it. We sense it in climate reports that come faster and faster, in burning forests and overheated seas. We see it in the gap between rich and poor that widens with every crisis, and in the invisible people for whom the world of work no longer has a place. We experience it in democracies that waver and in autocracies that grow stronger because trust has fallen apart. And we smell it in the digital fog of endless notifications, in algorithms that devour our time, steer our thoughts, and let our humanity fray.

This book is an attempt to draw a path that does not end at this wall. It is called SOLIS, and it is not a party programme, not a religion, and not a state. It is the design for a social order that can withstand the conditions of this century: an order that remains just even when the people in it are fallible, that emerges strengthened from crises, and that can correct itself. Before the design begins, it must be clear why it is needed.

The Dead End of the Established Orders

In the course of its history, humanity has produced several great designs for order, and each of them achieved something real.

Capitalism released prosperity and inventiveness on an unprecedented scale. At the same time, it plundered the natural foundations of life and divided societies. Socialism took the promise of equality seriously and, in the form it actually took, stifled freedom and the spirit of invention. Democracy gave people a voice, and it reaches its limit where many voices turn into a noise without direction that lacks the mechanisms of self-correction. Autocracy demonstrated resolve and order, at the expense of dignity and of the creative power without which no society stays alive.

None of these designs is simply wrong. Each has reached an inner limit that it cannot cross by its own logic. And these limits stand out so sharply today because the conditions under which those orders arose have shifted fundamentally. The great challenges of the present are no longer national, no longer separate from one another, and no longer slow. Climate change, the concentration of wealth, the upheaval brought by digital technology, and the erosion of a shared relation to truth are interlocked. They reinforce one another and unfold faster than established institutions can process them. An order built for a slower and more bounded world can no longer reliably steer a connected and accelerated one. We are not dealing with a broken cog that could be replaced. We are dealing with a machine whose design no longer fits the conditions.

Against this background, SOLIS does not see itself as one more ideology. It is the answer to a sober analysis of what is missing, and this answer can be traced back to four necessities.

First, we need an order that brings people, the planet, and technology into balance instead of subordinating one to another: neither humans above nature, nor the machine above humans, nor the economy above everything. Second, we need an order that does not maximize short-term profit but is aimed at durability, resilience, and shared well-being. Growth that consumes the foundations of its own possibility is not progress. Third, we need an order that promotes invention without sacrificing its ethical limits. It places the most powerful tools of the present, from artificial intelligence to genetic engineering, in the service of people instead of delivering people up to them. Fourth, we need an order that brings competence and conscience together, not through coercion, money, or the accumulation of power, but through meaning and a shared direction. The deepest upheavals of history were never merely technical. They were always cultural and ethical movements as well.

The objection is obvious that such a design is utopian. Yet it does not rest on the assumption of a better humanity. It rests on the insight that carrying on as before is the real improbability. In a world whose ecological, technological, and geopolitical fate is shared, extending the status quo is not the safe option but the riskiest of all. SOLIS builds on the resilient elements of human experience, on inventiveness, ethical depth, and the ability to think beyond what is immediately one’s own, and joins them into an order that holds.

Humanity at the Crossroads

Never before has humanity had so much power over its own fate. We can alter genes, leave the planet, turn the world into a single conversation, and use or destroy resources on a scale that was unthinkable only a few decades ago. And never before have we stood so close to the abyss, because what we gained in power we did not develop alongside it in wisdom, care, and public spirit. That the established orders are reaching their limits is therefore no abstract diagnosis. It shows itself in several simultaneous crises, each of which weighs heavily on its own and which only together, in their coupling, make up the real picture.

The ecological crisis is the most fundamental, because it concerns the material precondition of every society. An economy that consumes resources faster than they renew and treats the stability of the climate as a free dowry undermines the conditions of its own survival. No prosperity and no progress can be secured for long on a destabilized planet. The social crisis shows itself in a concentration of wealth and opportunity that hollows out the cohesion of societies. Where the gap between participation and exclusion opens too wide, the promise of equality before the law loses its tangible basis, and with it the trust that carries every order. The technological crisis is of its own kind, because it changes not only the means but people themselves. Digital systems today determine what people perceive, how they judge, and where they direct their attention. Where these systems serve only the exploitation of attention, a silent steering arises that escapes conscious control.

The political crisis follows from the others. Where institutions can no longer cope with the problems, trust dwindles. Where trust dwindles, simple answers gain power, answers that do not solve difficult situations but cover them up. Stable democracies begin to waver, and authoritarian models gain appeal because they promise the capacity to act. The crisis of meaning, finally, is the least visible and yet has the deepest effect. An order that addresses people above all as consumers, labour, and data points leaves a question unanswered that cannot be silenced: what all of this is for. We no longer know what is enough, what is good, and what “we” means. Consumption replaces meaning, distraction replaces engagement, efficiency replaces humanity. Without sound answers to the question of meaning, the inner hold from which societies draw their resilience falls apart.

These crises cannot be solved one at a time or one after another, because they condition one another. This is precisely the reason for a design cast from a single mould: an order that treats ecology, the economy, law, education, culture, and technology as separate departments will fail at the interfaces, where the real problems lie. SOLIS therefore does not start from a single reform, but from the connections between them.

The Five Ethical Foundations

Every lasting order needs a foundation that lies deeper than its laws, its technology, and its institutions, because these change while the foundation has to bear the load. It is made not of concrete, not of money, and not of power, but of values. In SOLIS there are five foundations. They are introduced here not as a creed but as working principles: each of them rules out certain orders and brings certain institutional consequences with it.

The Dignity of the Human Being

At the centre stands the human being as a value in themselves, not as a means: regardless of origin, sex, age, conviction, or ability to perform. It does not follow that all people are the same, but it does follow that they are equal in worth. An order that takes this principle seriously may not divide people into useful and useless ones and may not drop anyone because they are considered unproductive. A ninety-year-old woman with dementia is not a burden. She is part of the community, with a right to care. A refugee is not a cost factor. He is a person with a history, pain, and hope. The institutional consequence is a basic provision that does not depend on a person’s employment record, and a law that protects the weak as well as the strong.

The Balance of Individual and Community

The human being is neither an isolated lone fighter nor a mere element of the collective. They are a social individual who needs freedom in order to grow and community in order to flourish. The freedom of the individual ends where it destroys the good of the community. The good of the community may not be defined in a way that crushes the dignity of the individual. This balance is not a state but a living dance that has to be recalibrated again and again. It requires institutions that carry out exactly this weighing instead of deciding it one-sidedly. An enterprise may not circumvent environmental standards to earn more in the short term, because that harms the community. But neither may the community decide over people’s heads what is good for everyone, without participation, dialogue, and respect.

Responsibility for the Planet

The Earth is neither a backdrop nor a store of raw materials. It is the living world on which every society depends: a living system that we do not rule but to which we belong. From this follows the commitment to a use that allows renewal, to the greatest caution in interventions in ecosystems, and to the protection of the natural foundations as a core task rather than a side issue. This principle is not a moral add-on but a condition of survival: no system, no prosperity, and no progress can endure on a dying planet. It explains why the natural foundations in SOLIS are withdrawn from private appropriation and why the living world receives a voice of its own in the decisions of the order.

The Advancement of Knowledge and Wisdom

SOLIS bases its decisions not on power, money, or ideology, but on the best available knowledge, joined with ethical judgment. Knowledge without ethics can be destructive, and power without knowledge is blind. Only their combination holds. From this follows the commitment to ongoing research, to the testability of assumptions, and to open access to knowledge. And from this follows the insight that cleverness alone is not enough. It takes the foresight that considers consequences across generations, and the humility to hold one’s own error possible. Together, we call these two wisdom.

Long-Term Resilience

The worth of an order proves itself not in calm times but in crisis. SOLIS is therefore built for resilience. It is able to learn, because it can correct itself. It is strong, because it is organized in a decentralized way. And it is adaptable, because it takes feedback seriously. A health system that survives an epidemic without collapsing. An energy grid that absorbs the failure of one part with the others. A public sphere that recognizes a lie before it takes effect. That is what resilience looks like. In a future shaped by ecological, geopolitical, and technological uncertainty, this order is meant not merely to survive. It is meant to emerge from strain stronger than before.

These five foundations form the standard against which every following chapter can be measured. They are the compass that aligns individual decisions, and at the same time the condition for many individual rules becoming an order rather than a patchwork. And they are more than rules for institutions. They are also an attitude that people can grow into: respect for all life, truthfulness in word and deed, responsibility for one’s own actions, protection of the weak, and the recognition of the other’s freedom as the condition of one’s own. We are not building a house of cards. We are building a foundation that is meant to hold in a hundred and in five hundred years: a living system that can grow, learn, fail, renew itself, and rise again and again.

The Narrow Core and the Wide Freedom

One misunderstanding needs to be cleared up from the start. That SOLIS rests on five non-negotiable foundations does not mean that it prescribes to people what their good life consists of. The foundations are a narrow, firm core. Above it lies a great freedom. What meaning, happiness, and fulfilment are, and which paths lead to them, is decided not by the order but by each person and each community for themselves. An order that laid this down as well would no longer be a framework but a worldview that raises itself to law. SOLIS therefore strictly separates what applies to everyone from what belongs to each person: a narrow ground of the non-negotiable and, above it, the breadth of difference. This separation recurs throughout the whole work, from culture to the world order, and it is the reason why SOLIS does not fear diversity but needs it.

Method and Structure of the Work

The ambition of this work calls for a particular method, and it makes sense to disclose it before the individual areas are unfolded.

The first commitment is the link between principle and institution. A value that is not translated into a verifiable rule, a procedure, or an institution remains without consequence. This work is therefore not content with describing desirable conditions. As far as it can, it names the mechanisms through which they last, also and especially in case individual participants try to exploit the order. Wherever a principle is introduced, one must therefore always ask how it is secured. The second commitment is the application of the order to itself. The hardest question for any social order is not how it governs its citizens, but how it limits its own power. SOLIS does not answer it with a supreme supervisory body that would itself remain unsupervised. It applies the same principles to its own institutions: transparency, separation of powers, accountability to the citizenry, protection against capture. This idea carries the chapter on the order of power and recurs throughout the whole work. The third commitment is honesty about the design itself. Where a solution is only an approximation, the work says so. Where a path can fail, it names the danger.

On this basis, the structure unfolds. This first chapter lays the ethical foundation. The second sets out the order of power: fundamental rights, the Ring of institutions, and the public sphere. The third deals with law, the fourth with security, defence, and peace, the fifth with the economy and resources, the sixth with the planet. Then come education and knowledge, health and well-being, culture and meaning, and technology, artificial intelligence, and humanity’s place in the larger scheme of things. The eleventh chapter is devoted to children and families as the foundation on which every lasting change rests, the twelfth to the path from what exists to this order, the thirteenth to the relationship of SOLIS to the world. A final chapter sums up the foundations and building blocks and looks ahead.

A woman named Mara also walks through chapters two to thirteen. She is invented, and so is the town by the river where she lives. But everything that happens to her is exactly what this book designs. That is why she stands at the beginning of every chapter: so that the order is not only understood but seen.

The areas build on one another, but each can also be understood on its own, so that the work can be read from beginning to end or explored selectively. Not everyone thinks in the same order: some come by way of the environment, some by way of education, some by way of the question of meaning. The work is therefore a web, not a scaffold, and yet in the end it forms a single picture. It is not a text meant to reassure. It is a design meant to be argued with, written for all who believe that something other than the administration of what exists is possible: a fundamentally different and at the same time robust order.
