Chapter 02

Society and the Order of Power

Fundamental Rights and How They Are Weighed

At the heart of SOLIS lies a twofold promise: the individual is free, and the individual is protected — part of a community that neither crushes them nor leaves them to fend for themselves. The promise sounds self-evident, and yet existing orders often fail at exactly this point. They guarantee rights that are complete on paper and empty in real life: a right to education where the schools are falling apart; a right to health that depends on wealth; a freedom of expression silenced by economic or algorithmic power. SOLIS draws a fundamental conclusion from this: rights are only as strong as their practical safeguards. It is not enough to proclaim them; they must be tangible, measurable, and enforceable in everyday life.

The fundamental rights that SOLIS guarantees include the right to life and to physical and psychological integrity; freedom of expression, belief, and conscience; freedom of movement, of assembly, and of choice in education and occupation; and secure access to the material foundations of a dignified life — water, food, housing, education, health, and participation. This last guarantee is not an act of charity but the condition for all the other freedoms: there is no freedom in starving, and no freedom in dying of a treatable illness for lack of means. Beyond human beings, SOLIS recognizes that the natural world we share has its own claim to protection; the reasoning behind this is developed in the chapter on the economy and resources.

So that these rights do not remain abstract, they are anchored in procedures and institutions, and their actual state is continually assessed. An independent fundamental-rights monitor regularly examines how things really stand with people’s freedom and dignity — not in the letter of the law, but in everyday reality. Where it finds that particular groups are systematically disadvantaged, targeted programmes are triggered to reduce the inequality. Guaranteeing a right thus includes the duty to verify that it is fulfilled.

The hardest case is not the violation of a single right, but a conflict between two rights. Here it is not enough to appeal to wise judgment in the individual case, because that simply hands the real decision to the discretion of whoever makes it. SOLIS replaces this discretion with a procedure that has fixed limits. First, there is an absolute floor: human dignity and a person’s physical and psychological integrity are not open to weighing. They form a threshold below which no good may be traded off against another; no appeal to the common good and no gain in efficiency justifies violating them. Above this threshold, conflicts are decided by a regulated standard of proportionality. Whoever wants to restrict a right bears the burden of justification — not the person whose right would be restricted. The mildest effective means must always be chosen. The decision must be justified publicly, is subject to review by the competent bodies — the ethics councils and the courts — and is recorded as a precedent, so that like cases are treated alike in future. Weighing thus becomes a transparent procedure with a firm floor, a clear burden of proof, and the possibility of review, rather than a matter of good will.

The Order of Power: the Ring

One problem recurs throughout the history of every form of rule: power tends to preserve itself, to strengthen itself, and to protect itself. It attracts money, influence, and networks of loyalty; it forms closed circles in which interests cover for one another. In the end, the integrity of institutions, the trust of citizens, and society’s long-term capacity to act all suffer. An order that does not solve this problem, but merely moves it somewhere else, is not viable.

Why the Rule of the Wise Fails. The obvious answer is to put the wisest and most upright people at the top, chosen through a careful procedure that tests for wisdom, lifetime achievement, and strength of character. This answer is seductive, and history has refuted it. Plato’s philosopher-kings, the Chinese mandarinate, the Leninist vanguard — each of these designs began with the promise of selecting the best and ended in the concentration of power. The reason lies not in the failings of individuals but in a structural weakness: wisdom and integrity cannot be measured objectively. Where an order makes them a criterion for selection, the selection procedure itself becomes the most valuable prize — and thus the target of capture. Whoever controls the filter controls the system. The demand that “we simply choose good people” is therefore not a solution to the problem of power, but a way of moving it to a place that is harder to control. SOLIS draws a clear conclusion from this: instead of a more flattering description of those who govern, what is needed is a hard mechanism that withstands the very dynamic the order itself identifies. That mechanism is simple: there is no top.

The Ring instead of the Summit. In SOLIS, there is no president or monarch at the top, no party and no machine. Power is not organized as a pyramid but as a ring: a circle of bodies that scrutinize one another, none of which is the final authority.

The Council of the Wise is one of these bodies, but not their summit. Its task is long-term, cross-disciplinary orientation — thinking beyond the horizon of the day’s questions and supplying the other bodies with proposals and considerations. It advises, drafts, and warns; it does not command, and it does not decide alone. Its strength lies in judgment, not in reach.

The ethics councils act at every level — local, regional, and overarching — as bodies of normative review. They examine legislation and far-reaching decisions not only for legal and economic coherence, but for compatibility with the five foundations. They are drawn from many disciplines and hold strong powers of review and a suspensive veto: they can halt developments that fundamentally violate the values of the order — which gives them influence, not unlimited power.

The courts apply the law bindingly and equally to all. Crucially, they also apply it to the other bodies of the Ring; no office and no institution stands above the law.

Finally, an independent body for transparency and review secures the foundation without which the Ring cannot function: open access to information. It ensures that decisions, financial flows, and conflicts of interest can be traced, and it prevents any body from keeping the others in the dark by holding knowledge to itself. These bodies check one another in a circle. None can act without the others, none stands above the others, and there is no point at which the chain of control ends. This is exactly where the break with the pyramid lies: where there is no top, there is no ultimate target for capture.

The Citizens as the Outer Anchor. A ring that only turns in on itself would remain a closed system. The Ring is therefore anchored below, in the people. The citizens are the one node that sits outside the institutional circle, and they hold constituent power: they can recall the bodies of the Ring, overrule them, and appoint them anew. The members of the Council of the Wise and of the other bodies serve on a revocable mandate; their authority is borrowed, not owned.

Here, however, lies a danger that SOLIS deliberately avoids. Pure, direct majority rule would bring back the very forces the order rejects from the outset: populism, manipulation, echo chambers, a clamour of voices without direction. SOLIS already has the answer built in. The power of the citizens is not exercised as a raw vote, but structured through the deliberative system of participation: informed, grounded in facts, and protected against manipulation. Before every far-reaching decision, citizens receive vetted and diverse information, access to expert forums, and tools for ethical deliberation; only on this basis is a decision made. The people’s power thus remains the root of legitimacy without tipping over into populism.

In this way, two things come together that would not hold on their own. Power is a ring without a summit, the Ring is grounded in the people, and the people’s power is filtered through deliberation. This is the order applied to itself: SOLIS governs itself by the same principles by which it lets society govern itself. The Mechanics against Capture. So that all this remains a mechanism and not just a principle, the Ring rests on four concrete components. The first is selection by lot. Part of the oversight bodies is neither elected nor appointed, but filled by lot from among the citizens, in rotation, on the model of the ancient Athenian council or the jury. A lot cannot be bribed in advance; no career can be built on it; no stable cartel can form around it. It is the incorruptible element of the system.

The second is mandatory rotation with term limits. Power passes through many hands instead of settling in one place. Where no one stays in a post long enough to make it their own, nothing hardens. The third is the right of recall and reconstitution. The Council of the Wise and every other body can be dissolved and newly filled by a structured, informed majority of the citizens. The guardians serve as long as their mandate holds, and no longer.

The fourth is the separation of control over information from the power of decision. No body may both determine what is known and decide what is done. The body for transparency and review ensures that the base of knowledge is shared and open, so that no node can dominate the others through a monopoly on information.

Selecting the Council of the Wise. These components also answer the question on which the rule of the wise founders: how can the Council of the Wise be filled without a hidden filter for wisdom becoming the object of capture? SOLIS does without any body that judges the wisdom of candidates behind closed doors. Instead, it uses a multi-stage, open procedure. Candidates are nominated broadly and examined publicly — against disclosed and contestable standards: demonstrable judgment, lifetime achievement, and a proven capacity for responsibility, not an opaque score for “wisdom.” The decisive role in this procedure falls to a citizens’ panel chosen by lot, which itself rotates and therefore cannot be bought in advance. Final constituent power and the power of recall remain with the citizens. Terms are limited, mandates revocable. Wisdom is thus not certified once and for all; the Council remains continually accountable and replaceable.

An Order that Holds with Fallible People. The decisive test of an order of power is not whether it works with good people, but whether it remains just with fallible, self-interested ones. The Ring is built on this assumption. It does not require officeholders to be wise and selfless; it requires only that no one can entrench themselves permanently. A circle without a summit, whose bodies are partly chosen by lot, rotate, can be recalled, and operate in the open, remains correctable even when individuals try to exploit it. SOLIS thus solves the question of power not by hoping for better people, but through a structure that withstands the human temptation to power — and it is exactly this that distinguishes a resilient design from a mere vision.

Law and Justice

A society is only as just as its law. Values give direction; without institutions that declare and enforce the law, they remain theory. In many existing systems, people experience the justice system as impenetrable and over-bureaucratic, as dependent on money and influence, and as geared toward punishment rather than repair. SOLIS counters this with a legal system that puts balance, not power, at its centre, and that joins ethics and law in a dual structure: the ethics councils formulate guidelines and examine whether laws are compatible with the foundations; the legal institutions apply the rules bindingly and equally to all. The result is an order that is not only formally correct but also morally grounded.

Law in SOLIS is accessible. It may not be a secret code that only experts can decipher. The essential rules, rights, and procedures are available in plain language, taught in schools, and easy to find online, so that anyone caught up in a conflict can see, without a long search, which rights they have, which steps are open to them, and where to find help. Law in SOLIS is also transparent: no judgment is handed down without a public justification, no procedure runs without reviewable standards. Those who judge disclose the criteria by which they decide, so that the people involved can understand why something was decided, even when they dislike the outcome.

In dealing with wrongdoing, SOLIS leaves the logic of retribution behind. Harmful conduct has consequences, but the emphasis lies on restitution, healing, and addressing root causes. Someone who causes harm is not simply locked away, but drawn into a process that helps them grasp the consequences of their actions and involves them in putting things right; a company that breaches ecological standards does not just pay a fine but actively contributes to restoration. Repressive punishment often treats only symptoms; SOLIS targets the causes, because that is where lasting change lies.

Conflicts are resolved as early as possible and with the lowest possible threshold. The legal system is tiered: first come mediation and dialogue, only then the courts, and finally a last instance for questions of principle. A dispute between neighbours is settled by trained mediators before it escalates into a court case. The system is carried by judges and mediators who do not rise by the logic of career or power, but undergo a thorough ethical and psychological assessment, keep training throughout their careers, and are regularly reviewed; for a legal system is only as strong as the integrity, judgment, and empathy of those who apply it.

Above all stands the principle that ties the law to the order of power: no one is above the law — no officeholder and no institution, not even the Council of the Wise and not the ethics councils. Separation of powers, oversight, and review prevent arbitrary rule. Law is thus not a tool of the powerful but a shield for the weak: no class justice in which wealth buys its way out; no impenetrable procedures without justification; no law that only punishes instead of addressing the causes.

The Public Sphere, the Media, and the Question of Who Defines the Truth

Where societies fall apart, the collapse is almost always preceded by a loss: the loss of a shared grip on reality. Without a functioning public sphere that is both free and responsible, people no longer know what they can rely on; power elites shape the narratives unchecked, facts become weapons, and citizens drift either into indifference or into fanaticism. SOLIS counters this with a media order that is free not only in the legal sense, but also ethically grounded, transparent, diverse, and accessible.

This order steers between two distortions and avoids both. In authoritarian systems, the media become tools of control; in purely profit-driven systems, they become machines for harvesting attention, putting ratings above substance and division above understanding; and in the digital public sphere, monopolies of opinion arise that use algorithms to decide what gets seen. SOLIS therefore has neither state-controlled media nor a market that follows profit maximization alone. Media organizations work according to a verifiable ethical code; their ownership structures, financial flows, and conflicts of interest must be disclosed, because independence exists only where the media are free and at the same time accountable.

This raises the hardest question for any open society, and SOLIS does not dodge it. A free public sphere must be able to defend itself against targeted disinformation and manipulation that undermine democratic processes. Yet any authority with the power to decide what counts as false becomes the greatest danger in its own right — the gateway through which authoritarian encroachment has historically entered. The answer therefore cannot lie in a ministry of truth that rules on what is true and false, but only in a procedure.

This procedure rests on several commitments. There is no state censorship in advance. The standard response to false or harmful statements is counter-speech, context, and labelling, not removal. Where an intervention is nevertheless considered, it follows an adversarial procedure: a review panel partly chosen by lot decides; the burden of proof lies with the claim that something is false or manipulative; the reasoning is public; and the decision can be appealed. Above all stands an immovable limit: this procedure may never be used against criticism of SOLIS itself, of its institutions, or of its officeholders. Political dissent is protected without exception. It is precisely this limit that prevents the necessary defence against manipulation from becoming a tool for holding on to power. Transparency applies not only to the media, but equally to state and economic actors. Decisions, financial flows, and conflicts of interest must be open to inspection; lobbying registers are mandatory and public; citizens have the right to know who influenced a decision, which data it was based on, and which alternatives were considered. Finally, digital platforms, as the central arenas of today’s public debate, are treated as part of the democratic infrastructure and not as a purely private matter: their algorithms must be disclosed and open to review, dissenting voices must have fair access, and the platforms too are bound by the procedures and protective limits described above.

The deepest safeguard, however, lies not in any body that intervenes, but in education. A population that has learned from an early age to check sources, compare perspectives, and recognize manipulation is the strongest defence against a distorted public sphere — and makes any intervening authority as unnecessary as possible. The aim of the media order is therefore not perfect control of information, but a mature society that refuses to be manipulated, judges critically and with confidence, and knows how to argue without tearing itself apart.

The Guardians and the Instruments

Particular vigilance is owed to the measures by which the order gauges its own condition. The protection of fundamental rights can be monitored, yet every metric that becomes a target loses its meaning over time: where a number decides whether the order is judged healthy, the temptation grows to serve the number rather than the reality behind it. The hollowing-out then happens not through open attack but silently, while the gauge still reports good health. SOLIS responds in two ways: the measures themselves rotate, are set adversarially, and are deliberately called into question; and alongside measurable monitoring stands an unpredictable review carried out by citizens, which no one can prepare for. In this way, not only the guardians are watched, but the instruments too.

How the Order Changes

For a learning order to change without losing itself, it needs a regulated way of changing its own rules. SOLIS distinguishes two levels for this. The non-negotiable core — the five foundations, the fundamental rights, and the load-bearing safeguards against capture — can be touched only under high hurdles: with a large majority, after extensive deliberation, with a waiting period, and across generations. Everything else can move more freely, through the deliberative system of participation. This joins two things that would otherwise pull apart: the flexibility without which an order ossifies, and the firmness without which it gives itself up.

The Right to Leave

The maturity this work grants its citizens includes the right not only to reject the order from within, but to leave it. No one who does not want to live under the five foundations is forced to stay. The right to leave is itself a safeguard: an order that people are free to leave has to convince them through what it achieves, not by bolting the exit. How this right is organized without becoming a lever for destroying the common good is discussed in the chapter on world order.
